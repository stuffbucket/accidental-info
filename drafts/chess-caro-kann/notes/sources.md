## 1. What the Caro-Kann is, and its idea

Wikipedia, "Caro–Kann Defence". <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caro–Kann_Defence>
Page date: none shown (permalink oldid 1381151588). Cited works named in the footnotes include
Lasker 2005 and Hooper and Whyld 1996.

- The moves: "a chess opening beginning with the moves: 1. e4 c6"
- The idea: "Black prepares to contest the centre with 2...d5."
- Main line: "The main line is 2.d4 d5"
- Solid: "it is thought to be more solid and less dynamic than the Sicilian."
- The bishop, vs the French: "Unlike the similarly motivated French Defence, the Caro–Kann
  does not hinder the development of Black's queen's bishop."
- Not stated: that c6 "supports" d5. Chess.com (below) covers the idea in plain words.

Chess.com, "Caro-Kann Defense" (openings page). <https://www.chess.com/openings/caro-kann-defense>
Page date: none given (footer © 2026).

- "The Caro-Kann begins with 1.e4 c6, with most variations continuing 2.d4 d5."
- "It prepares for 2...d5 without blocking in the light-squared bishop"
- "which is considered the main drawback of its cousin the French Defense (1.e4 e6)"
- "The Caro-Kann Defense is known above all for its solidity."
- Pros: "Sound pawn structure," "Free light-squared bishop," "Safe and solid"
- Cons: "Less space for Black," "Slower development"

## 2. The light-squared bishop out before ...e6

Wikipedia, "French Defence". <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Defence>

- "The French Defence is a chess opening characterised by the moves: 1. e4 e6"
- "the pawn on e6 can impede the development of the bishop on c8, known by many players as the
  "French bishop"."
- Drawback: "the queen's bishop, which is blocked in by the pawn on e6 and can remain passive
  throughout the game."

Wikibooks, "Chess Opening Theory/1. e4/1...c6/2. d4/2...d5/3. e5/3...Bf5".
<https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Chess_Opening_Theory/1._e4/1...c6/2._d4/2...d5/3._e5/3...Bf5>

- "Black develops their bishop so that they may continue ...e6 to unleash their other bishop
  without restricting this one."

Chess.com openings page (above), Classical: "Black very reasonably develops the light-squared
bishop, which is why the move ...e6 has been avoided"

## 3. Advance Variation, 3.e5 Bf5

Wikipedia, "Caro–Kann Defence":

- "2.d4 d5 3.e5 Bf5, where White immediately secures a space advantage"
- "4.Nf3 e6 5.Be2 c5 6.Be3, popularised by English GM Nigel Short" (name not for the guide)

Wikibooks, 3...Bf5 page (above):

- "Once Black has played ...e6, their king's bishop supports the 5...c5 pawn break"
- "The main line is 4. Nf3, the Short variation, 4...e6 5. Be2."
- The bishop "is unsupported and potentially vulnerable on f5."
- "4. h4, the Tal variation, is a more direct way of attacking Black's bishop." "More common
  is 4...h5 5. Bd3 Bxd3 6. Qxd3 e6="
- TRAP: "If 4...e6?? the bishop cannot be saved: 5. g4 Be4 6. f3 Bg6 7. h5±"
- "4. Nc3 is the sharp van der Wiel attack." "4...e6 5. g4 Bg6 6. Nge2 c5 7. h4 (threatening
  8. h5 winning the bishop) h5"

Wikibooks, "Chess Opening Theory/1. e4/1...c6/2. d4/2...d5".
<https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Chess_Opening_Theory/1._e4/1...c6/2._d4/2...d5>

- 3.e5: "White closes the centre and takes more space."
- 3.exd5: "Black retakes 3...cxd5 and frees the c6 for their knight again."
- 3.Nc3 and 3.Nd2: "prepare to recapture with the knight. Usually, both moves reach the same
  position after 3...dxe4 4. Nxe4."

Chess.com openings page: "3.Nc3 is White's most common choice by a narrow margin over 3.e5."

## 4. Exchange Variation, 3.exd5 cxd5

Wikipedia, "Caro–Kann Defence":

- "The main line of the Exchange Variation begins with 3.exd5 cxd5 4.Bd3."
- "Bd3 prevents ...Bf5 while still developing."
- "The most common continuation is 4...Nc6 5.c3 Nf6 6.Bf4 Bg4 7.Qb3."
- Panov: "The Panov–Botvinnik Attack begins with the move 4.c4."

Wikibooks, 3.exd5 page. <https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Chess_Opening_Theory/1._e4/1...c6/2._d4/2...d5/3._exd5>

- "White exchanges on d5, and black almost always responds with cxd5."

Chess.com: "When White plays 4.Bd3 the goal is a quiet game."

## 5. Main line, 3.Nc3 (or 3.Nd2) dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5

Chess.com openings page:

- "White can also reach these lines with 3.Nd2 when 3...dxe4 4.Nxe4 transposes."
- "4...Bf5 is Black's most popular, and this is the Classical Variation."
- "White can, and often does, chase the bishop, but this weakens the kingside."

Wikibooks, 4...Bf5 page. <https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Chess_Opening_Theory/1._e4/1...c6/2._d4/2...d5/3._Nc3/3...dxe4/4._Nxe4/4...Bf5>

- "Black develops their bishop and attacks White's knight on e4."
- "White often sticks to the main line with 5. Ng3, forcing the Bf5 back."

Wikipedia, "Caro–Kann Defence":

- "White usually continues: 5.Ng3 Bg6 6.h4 h6 7.Nf3 Nd7 8.h5 Bh7 9.Bd3 Bxd3 10.Qxd3."
- "plays a timely c6–c5 to contest the d4-square."

Wikibooks, 6.h4 page. <https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Chess_Opening_Theory/1._e4/1...c6/2._d4/2...d5/3._Nc3/3...dxe4/4._Nxe4/4...Bf5/5._Ng3/5...Bg6/6._h4>

- "Black needs to defend the bishop, and h6 is almost universal"

## 6. Traps

Smothered mate. Wikipedia, "Smothered mate". <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smothered_mate>

- "There is also a well-known trap in the Caro–Kann Defence:"
- "1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 dxe4 4. Nxe4 Nd7 5. Qe2!? Ngf6?? 6. Nd6#"
- "This trap has occurred in many games."
- Definition: "a checkmate delivered by a knight in which the mated king is unable to move"

Wikipedia, "Caro–Kann Defence": "the quick mating trap for White, 5.Qe2 Ngf6?? 6.Nd6#
(smothered mate)" ... "but after 5...Ndf6, Black enjoys a solid position."

The trapped bishop in the Advance: Wikibooks 3...Bf5 page, section 3 above ("If 4...e6?? the
bishop cannot be saved: 5. g4 Be4 6. f3 Bg6 7. h5±").

The h-pawn in the main line: Wikibooks 6.h4 page, section 5 above.

- Not found: a public source tying Qb3 to an attack on b7 in the lines the guide teaches.
  Wikipedia only gives "7.Qb3" in the Exchange line. Left out.
