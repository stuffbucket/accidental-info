# Sources for An Accidental Guide to… Ordering Wine for the Table

All read on 22 October 2026. Excerpts are verbatim and short. Public sources: wine
publishers (Wine Spectator, Decanter, Wine Folly), one economics working paper, and the US
National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA). Names of people quoted inside
these articles stay out of the guide.

## 1. What "dry" means

Wine Spectator, Ask Dr. Vinny, "What is 'dry' wine? Aren't all wines wet …?" 12 July 2022.
<https://www.winespectator.com/articles/what-is-dry-wine-aren-t-all-wines-wet>

- "When all the perceptible sugar in a wine is converted to alcohol, it is considered 'dry.'"
- "here the opposite of dry is not wet, but sweet."
- "Dry wines won't necessarily dry out your mouth—they're just not sweet"
- A second sense: "It's about the perception of dryness, or how a wine feels in your mouth." (tannin)
- Not stated: any grams-per-litre threshold. The guide gives none.

Wine Spectator, Ask Dr. Vinny, "What is an 'off-dry' wine?" 15 August 2018.
<https://www.winespectator.com/articles/what-is-off-dry-wine-56827>

- "an 'off-dry' or 'semi-dry' wine means that there's a bit of leftover sugar."
- "off-dry wine has a soft or mild sweetness to it"
- "My favorite examples of off-dry wines tend to be Rieslings." … "makes them an excellent match to spicy foods."

## 2. Grape names and body

Wine Folly (Madeline Puckette), "What Is Wine Body and How To Taste It". 16 November 2020,
modified 10 February 2025.
<https://winefolly.com/tips/what-is-wine-body-and-how-to-taste-it/>

- "Wine body is defined by how heavy and rich a wine tastes. It’s a combination of several factors: grape variety, alcohol level, and even sweetness level."
- "Alcohol Level: Wines above 14% alcohol tend to taste more full-bodied."
- Light-bodied red wines: "Pinot Noir", "Gamay". "Generally speaking, light-bodied red wines have average alcohol levels, lower tannins, and less color."
- Medium-bodied: "a Merlot with lower alcohol (under 14%) and less oak-aging may also be medium-bodied."
- Full-bodied red wines: "Malbec", "Syrah", "Cabernet Sauvignon".
- "one reason Chardonnay is often considered a full-bodied white wine is because of its frequent oak aging."

Decanter (Chris Mercer and Amanda Barnes), "How to order wine like a pro" (see 4):
- "Aromatic off-dry whites, such as Riesling and Gewürztraminer, would offer an easy pairing" (with curry).
- "Is the restaurant known for its oysters and shellfish? Dry sparkling and white wines are a good place to start."

## 3. The second-cheapest bottle

David de Meza and Vikram Pathania, "Is the Second-Cheapest Wine a Rip-Off? Economics vs.
Psychology in Product-Line Pricing". American Association of Wine Economists, Working Paper
No. 264, March 2021.
<https://wine-economics.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/AAWE_WP264.pdf>

- "Restaurateurs are believed to overprice the second-cheapest wine to exploit naïve diners embarrassed to choose the cheapest option."
- "We find that the mark-up on the second cheapest wine is significantly below that on the four next more expensive wines. It is an urban myth that the second-cheapest wine is an especially bad buy. Percentage mark-ups are highest on mid-range wines."
- "We sampled 249 London-based restaurants on Tripadvisor.co.uk in July 2015." "Of the sampled restaurants, 235 had "readable" wine menus"
- Status: "AAWE Working Papers are circulated for discussion and comment purposes. They have not been subject to a peer review process." A version was later published in Economics Letters (2021); the ScienceDirect page refused the fetch, so the guide cites the working paper.

## 4. Reading the list and talking to the server

Decanter (Chris Mercer, with tips by Amanda Barnes), "How to order wine like a pro".
Published 7 May 2021, updated 12 April 2022.
<https://www.decanter.com/learn/how-to-order-wine-like-a-pro-395777/>

- Second-cheapest: "found no evidence to support a long-held ‘urban myth’ of the second cheapest wine being the most overpriced"
- "If you’re not sure about many of the names on the list, then don’t be afraid to speak to the sommelier or one of the team."
- "tell him or her what you’re eating, the styles of wine you like and don’t like, and give them an idea of your budget, perhaps with a price band to work within"
- "It’s always best to be upfront, to say “this is my budget” and to use the expertise of the people who are there"
- "Restaurants mark their wines up anywhere between 50% and 400%, although this varies across the list and between venues."
- Value: "When looking for value on a wine list, it can be a good idea to look for unusual regions or grape varieties"
- Mixed dishes: "If you’re going for a light fish dish for a starter and then a heavy lamb shank for a main course, it will be hard to find a bottle of wine to suit both. This is when you could explore the by-the-glass options."
- Large table: "If you are a large table, order a few bottles so that each guest can pair with their course accordingly."
- "Take your time, there’s no need to rush."
- "If your dinner companion looks thirsty, how about ordering a glass of house Champagne to kick off the evening? Sipping on bubbles, you can now take your time to muse over that wine list"
- "A sommelier is there to help. The more information you give, the easier it is for them to help you to make the right decision."
- Not stated: how a list is laid out section by section. The guide says only what these
  sources support.

## 5. The taste

Decanter (Laura Seal), "How to taste wine in a restaurant and survive the ritual".
Published 2 April 2017, updated 12 April 2022.
<https://www.decanter.com/learn/taste-wine-in-the-restaurant-364949/>

- "If you don’t check the producer, style and vintage you run the risk of forking out for a different, possibly inferior, wine."
- "Different vintages may have different prices"
- Cork: "Sniffing the cork is an option, but it’s generally thought not much can be detected from doing so."
- "If you are the host, the waiter will pour a little of the wine into your glass for you to taste."
- "This is the point at which you should raise any concerns with the sommelier — not once your guests have wincingly drunk half a glass of faulty wine"
- "The wine is offered for tasting so you can check it’s not corked"
- "If you think it’s smelling musty, mouldy or simply unaccountably flat — ask for it to be replaced."

Wine Folly (Madeline Puckette), "How to Order Wine at a Restaurant". No page date.
<https://winefolly.com/tips/how-to-order-wine-at-a-restaurant/>

- "verify the bottle, inspect the cork, and approve the wine sample."
- "he is simply verifying that the bottle he has in his hand is the wine that you ordered."
- "Approving the sample is simply to determine if the wine is flawed in any way."
- "A corked wine smells like wet cardboard and is super musty."
- "Once you determine that it's not flawed, tell the server to pour around!"

Wine Folly (Madeline Puckette), "Awkward Moments: Ordering Wine in a Restaurant". 13 May
2016, modified 16 August 2024.
<https://winefolly.com/lifestyle/awkward-moments-ordering-wine-in-a-restaurant/>

- "Today, the presentation of the cork is completely for show."
- On a wine you dislike: "if you’re spending under $100 on a bottle of wine, it’s more-or-less reasonable to let them know and go about finding an alternative." (One writer's view. The guide says to ask, not that it's a right.)

## 6. How many glasses in a bottle

NIAAA, Rethinking Drinking, "How Much Alcohol is in Beer, Wine & Liquor?" (How many drinks
are in common containers).
<https://rethinkingdrinking.niaaa.nih.gov/how-much-too-much/how-many-drinks-are-common-containers>

- Table wine (12% alc/vol): "750 ml (a regular wine bottle) = 5" (US standard drinks).

NIAAA, "What Is A Standard Drink?" Updated December 2025.
<https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/alcohols-effects-health/what-standard-drink>

- "A 5-ounce glass of wine at 12% alcohol by volume" (not used after EDITS-3: the guide says "standard drinks" from the containers page instead, so this page is not on the sources page)

## Where sources disagree

- Sending back a bottle you don't like: Decanter and Wine Folly agree the taste is for
  faults. Wine Folly adds that under $100 it's reasonable to say so and ask for another.
  The guide says the taste is for faults and that you can ask, kindly.
