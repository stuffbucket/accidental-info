Claims checked and supported, with no change needed: what dry means (line 90, Wine

## Overreach

### O1. The second-cheapest finding is an average, and “next four” is unclear (line 123, Price, Confused with)

**Guide:** “One study of 235 London wine lists found its markup was lower than the next four
bottles’ markups.”

**Source** (AAWE Working Paper 264,
https://wine-economics.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/AAWE_WP264.pdf): “We find that the
mark-up on the second cheapest wine is significantly below that on the four next more
expensive wines.” “We sampled 249 London-based restaurants on Tripadvisor.co.uk in July
2015.”

**Verdict:** overreach. The paper reports a statistical result across lists, not a rule that
holds on every list. “The next four bottles” could mean the next four printed, where the
paper means the next four up in price. The data are from 2015 (see S1).

**Corrected:** “In a study of 235 London lists from 2015, its markup was on average below the
next four wines up in price.”

### O2. Asking to replace a sound bottle you dislike (lines 142 to 143, Taste, Confused with)

**Guide:** “The taste tells you whether the wine is sound. If it’s sound and you don’t like
it, you can still ask kindly about another bottle.”

**Source** (Wine Folly, Awkward Moments,
https://winefolly.com/lifestyle/awkward-moments-ordering-wine-in-a-restaurant/): “if you’re
spending under $100 on a bottle of wine, it’s more-or-less reasonable to let them know and go
about finding an alternative.” `sources.md` marks this as one writer’s view.

**Verdict:** overreach. The guide drops the under-$100 limit and states one writer’s view as
general permission. Decanter and the other Wine Folly page say the taste is for faults only.

**Corrected:** “The taste is for faults. If a bottle under $100 has no fault but you don’t
like it, one wine site says it’s reasonable to ask for another.”

### O3. Body comes from more than the grape (line 70, Grape, What)

**Guide:** “Grapes differ in body, which is how heavy and rich a wine tastes.”

**Source** (Wine Folly, What Is Wine Body,
https://winefolly.com/tips/what-is-wine-body-and-how-to-taste-it/): “It’s a combination of
several factors: grape variety, alcohol level, and even sweetness level.” “Wines above 14%
alcohol tend to taste more full-bodied.”

**Verdict:** overreach. The guide makes the grape the cause of body. The source names the
grape as one of three factors.

**Corrected:** “Body is how heavy and rich a wine tastes. Grape and alcohol both affect it.”

### O4. Pinot Noir stated as always light (line 71, Grape, So what)

**Guide:** “A few names cover a lot. Pinot Noir is a light red and Cabernet Sauvignon a full
one, while Chardonnay is often a full white.”

**Source** (Wine Folly, What Is Wine Body): “Generally speaking, light-bodied red wines have
average alcohol levels, lower tannins, and less color.” “Wines above 14% alcohol tend to
taste more full-bodied.”

**Verdict:** overreach. The source lists Pinot Noir as a light red “generally speaking”, and
says high alcohol makes a wine fuller. “A few names cover a lot” has no source.

**Corrected:** “Ask for light or full. Pinot Noir is usually a light red and Cabernet
Sauvignon a full one, while Chardonnay is often a full white.”

### O5. “No” to smelling the cork (line 152, Common questions)

**Guide:** “No. Little can be learned from smelling it, and presenting the cork is mostly for
show.”

**Source** (Decanter, How to taste wine in a restaurant,
https://www.decanter.com/learn/taste-wine-in-the-restaurant-364949/): “Sniffing the cork is
an option, but it’s generally thought not much can be detected from doing so.”

**Verdict:** overreach. The source calls it an option, not something to avoid. “Mostly for
show” is softer than Wine Folly’s “completely for show”, which is reasonable because the
other Wine Folly page says to “inspect the cork”.

**Corrected:** “You don’t need to. Little can be learned from smelling it, and presenting the
cork is mostly for show.”

### O6. A price band rules out “most” of the list (line 111, Price, So what)

**Guide:** “A price band lets the server rule out most of the list.”

**Source** (Decanter, How to order wine like a pro,
https://www.decanter.com/learn/how-to-order-wine-like-a-pro-395777/): “The more information
you give, the easier it is for them to help you to make the right decision.”

**Verdict:** overreach. The source says a budget helps. It doesn’t say how much of the list it
removes.

**Corrected:** “A price band helps the server narrow the list.”

### O7. “Many Rieslings” (line 91, Dry, So what)

**Guide:** “Ask for off-dry if you’d like a little, as in many Rieslings.”

**Source** (Wine Spectator, What is an “off-dry” wine?,
https://www.winespectator.com/articles/what-is-off-dry-wine-56827): “My favorite examples of
off-dry wines tend to be Rieslings.” Decanter: “Aromatic off-dry whites, such as Riesling and
Gewürztraminer”.

**Verdict:** overreach, low. Both sources give Riesling as an example of off-dry. Neither
says how many Rieslings are off-dry.

**Corrected:** “Ask for off-dry if you’d like a little, as in some Rieslings.”

## Unsourced

### U1. How the list is laid out (line 35, How does ordering go?)

**Guide:** “Find that price, then look at whites, reds or sparkling wines.”

**Verdict:** unsourced. `sources.md` says “Not stated: how a list is laid out section by
section.” The line assumes the list is arranged so you can find a price first and then a
colour. Cut the layout claim.

**Corrected:** “Note the whites, reds or sparkling wines within your price band.”

### U2. The grape comes before the producer (line 69, Grape, Identify by)

**Guide:** “A name such as Pinot Noir before the producer or place.”

**Verdict:** unsourced. No source says where the grape name sits in a list entry. Cut the
position.

**Corrected:** “A name such as Pinot Noir or Chardonnay.”

### U3. A sparkling glass to buy time (line 149, Common questions)

**Guide:** “A glass of something sparkling to start gives you a few minutes.”

**Verdict:** unsourced. Decanter says only “Take your time, there’s no need to rush.” Cut the
sentence.

**Corrected:** “No. Take your time with the list.”

### U4. Why unusual wines are on the list (line 151, Common questions)

**Guide:** “Those wines are often on the list for their quality and value.”

**Verdict:** unsourced. Decanter says “it can be a good idea to look for unusual regions or
grape varieties”. It gives no reason they are on the list. The study has a sourced value fact
that fits here.

**Corrected:** “Look for less familiar regions and grapes. In the London study, percentage
markups were highest on mid-range wines.” (AAWE: “Percentage mark-ups are highest on
mid-range wines.”)

### U5. Chablis and Rioja (line 82, Grape, Confused with)

**Guide:** “Some wines are listed by place, such as Chablis or Rioja.”

**Verdict:** unsourced. Neither name appears in `sources.md`. Either add a source that names
them as regions, or drop the examples.

**Corrected:** “Some wines are listed by region instead of grape.” (See N2 for line 83.)

### U6. Tannin causes the drying feel (lines 98 and 103, Dry)

**Guide:** “A drying feel is tannin” and “which comes from tannin, not sugar.”

**Verdict:** unsourced, low. `sources.md` has “(tannin)” only as the compiler’s note after
the excerpt “how a wine feels in your mouth”. No verbatim line names tannin as the cause.
Either add the Wine Spectator sentence that names tannin to `sources.md`, or use the
corrected text, which rests on “Dry wines won’t necessarily dry out your mouth—they’re just
not sweet”.

**Corrected:** line 98: “A drying feel in the mouth”. Lines 102 to 103: “A dry wine isn’t
sweet. A wine can also feel drying in your mouth, which has nothing to do with sugar.”

Also unsourced, low: line 49 “pages at the back of the main menu” and line 109 “The column of
numbers beside the wines”. Both describe layout, which `sources.md` says it doesn’t cover. A
reader is unlikely to be misled. If you want them sourced, corrected line 49: “A menu of wine
names, each with a price.”

## Naming

### N1. “Price” means two things (line 25 against line 110)

**Guide:** line 25: “Price | The price band you set before reading any names.” Line 110:
“Price is what the restaurant charges for a bottle or glass.”

**Source** (Decanter, How to order wine like a pro): “give them an idea of your budget,
perhaps with a price band to work within”. Price band and price are different things in the
source too.

**Verdict:** naming. The terms table and the entry define the same term differently.

**Corrected:** line 25: “| Price | What the restaurant charges for a bottle or glass. |”

### N2. “Place” and “region” for one idea (lines 78, 82, 83)

**Guide:** “Place names instead of grapes” (line 78). “Some wines are listed by place”
(line 82). “which grape and style a place name means” (line 83). The heading is “Region”
(line 80) and line 151 says “regions”.

**Source** (Decanter): “look for unusual regions or grape varieties”.

**Verdict:** naming. The source and the guide’s own heading say region.

**Corrected:** line 78: “Regions instead of grapes”. Line 83: “Ask the server which grape and
style the region means.”

### N3. “Sound” is not the sources’ word (lines 39 and 142)

**Guide:** “Nod when the wine is sound” (line 39). “whether the wine is sound” (line 142).

**Source** (Wine Folly, How to Order Wine at a Restaurant): “Once you determine that it’s not
flawed, tell the server to pour around!” Decanter: “so you can check it’s not corked”.

**Verdict:** naming. The sources and the rest of the guide say faulty or flawed. “Sound” is
undefined trade usage.

**Corrected:** line 39: “Nod when the wine isn’t faulty, and the server will pour it.” For
line 142, see O2.

## Stale

**Guide:** “One study of 235 London wine lists”

**Source** (AAWE Working Paper 264): “We sampled 249 London-based restaurants on
Tripadvisor.co.uk in July 2015.”

**Verdict:** stale. The lists are eleven years old and the guide gives no date. The
corrected text in O1 adds “from 2015”.

**Corrected:** see O1.

### S2. The markup range has no date (line 110)

**Guide:** “Markups run from 50 to 400 percent and vary across the list.”

**Source** (Decanter, How to order wine like a pro, published May 2021, updated April 2022):
“Restaurants mark their wines up anywhere between 50% and 400%, although this varies across
the list and between venues.”

**Verdict:** stale, low. One article’s range from 2022, given in the present tense.

**Corrected:** “Markups run from 50 to 400 percent, by a 2022 estimate, and vary across the
list.”
