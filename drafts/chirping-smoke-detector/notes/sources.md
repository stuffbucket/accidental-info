# Sources for An Accidental Guide to… Smoke Detectors That Chirp

All fetched 22 October 2026. Excerpts are verbatim and short. Public, official sources only.

## 1. NFPA, "NFPA Educational Messages, 2026 Edition: Desk Reference for the Fire Service and Fire and Life Safety Educators"

<https://www.nfpa.org/-/media/project/storefront/catalog/files/home-fire-safety/emac/educationalmessagesdeskreference.pdf?rev=967564ad45c84627bb02b5baef0dc12c>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: 2026 edition ("© 2026 National Fire Protection Association"; PDF created 11 August 2026).

- Low-battery chirp timing, and what it means if the chirp continues after a battery change: "A single “chirp” every 30 or 60 seconds means the battery is low and must be changed. Chirping that continues after the battery has been replaced means the alarm is at the end of its life, and the alarm must be replaced."
- Replace at 10 years, date on the alarm: "Replace all smoke alarms when they are 10 years old from the manufacture date. The date can be found on the outside or back of the smoke alarm. If there is no date indicated, replace the alarm."
- Why alarms wear out: "Sensors in the alarm wear out over time. When this happens, they may not work as well and may take longer to detect and alert to a fire."
- End-of-life warning: "That is why smoke alarms should be replaced after 10 years or sooner if they give an end-of-life warning."
- Test monthly: "Test smoke alarms at least once a month using the test button."
- Replaceable batteries: "If your smoke alarm uses replaceable batteries, change the batteries at least once a year."
- Never disable: "Never remove batteries or disable a smoke or CO alarm." (This line is in the chapter for college and university students.)
- Interconnection: "For the best protection, interconnect all smoke alarms. When one sounds, they all sound."
- CO alarm end of life (low priority): "The sensors in CO alarms have a limited life. Replace the CO alarm according to manufacturer’s instructions or when the end-of-life signal sounds."
- CO low-battery signal: "Know the difference between the sound of the CO alarm and the smoke alarm and the low-battery signals. If the audible low-battery signal sounds, replace the batteries or replace the device."
- Not stated: how to find which alarm is chirping; LED patterns; hush or silence durations; why chirps start at night; resetting an alarm after a battery change; turning off the breaker before working on a hardwired alarm.

## 2. NFPA, "Learn the Sounds" (Fire Prevention Week handout: "What is your alarm telling you?")

<https://content.nfpa.org/-/media/Project/Storefront/Catalog/Files/Fire-Prevention-Week/2024/FPW24-Learn-the-Sounds.pdf?rev=f6242165a65f4a15bf4cd47aa3f30760>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: the file is in the Fire Prevention Week 2024 folder, but the footer reads "©2021 National Fire Protection Association".

- Smoke alarm sound (three beeps): "A continued set of three loud beeps—beep, beep, beep—means smoke or fire. Get out, call 9-1-1, and stay out."
- Low-battery chirp: "A single “chirp” every 30 or 60 seconds means the battery is low and must be changed."
- End of life: "Chirping that continues after the battery has been replaced means the alarm is at the end of its life and the unit must be replaced."
- Replace at 10 years: "All smoke alarms must be replaced after 10 years."
- CO alarm sound (four beeps): "A continuous set of four loud beeps—beep, beep, beep, beep—means carbon monoxide is present in your home. Go outside, call 9-1-1 and stay out."
- CO end of life varies: "CO alarms also have “end of life” sounds that vary by manufacturer. This means it’s time to get a new CO alarm."
- Check your own model: "Learn the sounds of your smoke and carbon monoxide alarms by checking the user guide or search the brand and model online."
- Not stated: how to find the chirping unit; hush; why chirps start at night; hardwired alarms; how many years before a CO alarm reaches end of life.

## 3. NFPA Research (Tucker McGree), "Smoke Alarms in US Home Fires"

<https://content.nfpa.org/-/media/Project/Storefront/Catalog/Files/Research/NFPA-Research/Detection-and-signaling/ossmokealarms.pdf?rev=40db028bf7d544fbbba4f403928dcc47>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: June 2024 (data years 2018–2022).

- Deaths with no working alarms: "Nearly three out of five (59 percent) home fire deaths were caused by fires in properties with no smoke alarms (43 percent) or smoke alarms that failed to operate (16 percent)."
- Why alarms fail: "Missing or non-functional power sources, including missing or disconnected batteries, dead batteries, and disconnected hardwired alarms, were the most common factors when smoke alarms failed to operate."
- Working alarms lower the death rate: "The death rate per 1,000 home structure fires is approximately 60 percent lower in homes with working smoke alarms than in homes with no alarms or none that operated."
- The night chirp leads people to disable alarms: "The low-battery chirp is sometimes considered a nuisance." / "In practice, even people committed to fire safety may disable an alarm that starts chirping in the middle of the night."
- Replacing batteries every year prevents chirps: "Replacing conventional batteries in battery-powered and hardwired alarms with battery backups annually reduces the likelihood of inconvenient chirping and the subsequent disabling of alarms."
- Don't swap a 10-year lithium battery for an ordinary one: "Reminders to change smoke alarm batteries when changing the clock for Daylight Saving Time might lead some people to replace a lithium battery with a conventional battery with a shorter lifespan."
- Not stated: chirp patterns; how to find or reset an alarm. The exact wording "Roughly three out of five home fire deaths result from fires in properties without smoke alarms or with no working smoke alarms" was not found. The wording above is the version on record. The 2015 NFPA installation guide uses similar wording ("three out of five home fire deaths result from fires in homes with no smoke alarms or…"), but that is older and is not listed here. The phrase "When smoke alarms fail to operate, it is usually because batteries are missing, disconnected or dead" was also not found word for word. The research report's sentence above is the citable version.

## 4. U.S. Fire Administration (FEMA), "Smoke alarms"

<https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prepare-for-fire/smoke-alarms/>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: "Page last reviewed: Oct. 13, 2026".

- A dead or missing battery is the same as no alarm: "A smoke alarm with a dead or missing battery is the same as having no smoke alarm at all."
- Power types: "If the smoke alarm is powered by battery, it runs on either a disposable 9-volt battery or a nonreplaceable 10-year lithium-ion (“long-life”) battery. Alarms that get power from your home's electrical system, or “hardwired” alarms, usually have a backup battery that will need to be replaced once a year."
- Replace at 10 years from the manufacture date: "They need to be replaced 10 years from the manufacture date."
- Maintenance by type, 9-volt: "Test the alarm monthly. Replace the batteries at least once every year. Replace the entire smoke alarm every 10 years."
- Maintenance by type, long-life: "Test the alarm monthly. Replace the entire smoke alarm according to the manufacturer's instructions."
- Maintenance by type, hardwired: "Test the alarm monthly. Replace the backup battery at least once every year. Replace the entire smoke alarm every 10 years."
- Do not remove the battery: "Disabling a smoke alarm or removing the battery can be a deadly mistake."
- Hardwired installs: "Only qualified electricians should install hardwired smoke alarms."
- Interconnection: "Smoke alarms should be interconnected. When one sounds, they all sound."
- Not stated: chirping, chirp timing, finding the chirping unit, why chirps start at night, resetting, hush durations, turning off the breaker.

## 5. First Alert (Resideo), "Why does your smoke alarm chirp in the middle of the night?"

<https://www.firstalert.com/blogs/safety-corner/why-does-your-smoke-alarm-chirp-in-the-middle-of-the-night>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: none shown on the page. The page metadata gives published 2 December 2025 and modified 15 December 2025.

- Causes: "Smoke alarms may chirp at night due to a low battery, or because the alarm is reaching its end of life, which is typically 10 years."
- Why it happens at night: "Smoke alarms can also chirp at night because home temperatures often drop several degrees, which can impact the chemical reactions inside the battery, reducing capacity."
- Mechanism: "This slowdown increases internal (ionic) resistance and causes a voltage drop inside the cell – which in turn means lower voltage at the cell terminals, resulting in a chirp."
- Other causes: "Other reasons smoke detectors may chirp at night can include dust or humidity, such as bathroom steam, interfering with the sensor."
- Not stated: a specific time of night; chirp timing; how to find or reset the alarm.

## 10. Kidde, "Why Do Smoke Alarms Always Chirp in the Middle of the Night?"

<https://www.kidde.com/support/smoke-alarms/nighttime-alarm-chirps>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: none given.

- The 3 a.m. chirp: "It's a sound many homeowners have heard: the 3 a.m. alarm chirp."
- Mechanism: "As a smoke alarm's battery nears the end of its life, the amount of power it produces causes an internal resistance. A drop in room temperature increases this resistance, which may impact the battery's ability to deliver the power necessary to operate the unit in an alarm situation."
- Timing: "Most homes are the coolest between 2 a.m. and 6 a.m. That's why the alarm may sound a low-battery chirp in the middle of the night, and then stop when the home warms up a few degrees."
- Replace at 10 years: "Kidde recommends replacing all smoke alarms—battery and hardwired—every 10 years. If your alarms are older or you don't know their age, you should replace them."
- CO end of life (low priority): "CO alarms will sound a required "end-of-life" chirp after seven to ten years of power."
- Prevention tip, which mentions weekly testing: "Replace the batteries in all your alarms, and remember to test them weekly."
- Not stated: chirp intervals; how to find the unit.

## 11. Kidde, "What Causes Intermittent Beeping or Chirping?"

<https://www.kidde.com/support/smoke-alarms/intermittent-beeping-causes>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: none given. The page's breadcrumb reads "Carbon monoxide alarms", but the URL is in the smoke-alarms section and the text covers smoke alarms.

- Why it can keep chirping after a new battery: "Sometimes, the alarm's processor retains error conditions, such as a weak battery, even after the battery is replaced."

- Reset for battery-only alarms: "Remove the battery. Press and hold the test button for at least 15 seconds. The unit may chirp or alarm for a few seconds before going silent. Reinstall the battery. The unit will chirp when the battery is first connected but should not chirp afterward."
- Loose battery: "A loose or improperly installed battery is a common cause of random beeping."
- Interconnected systems can confuse the search: "A loose white neutral wire can cause an echoing chirp from one unit to the next."
- Battery freshness: "Use a battery with a use-by date no later than four to five years in the future."
- Replacement age, and date code location: "Smoke and heat alarms should be replaced every 10 years, and carbon monoxide and combination alarms every 7 years. Check the date code on the label attached to the back of the unit."
- Not stated: chirp intervals; LED patterns.

## Where sources disagree
- **How often to test.** NFPA (source 1) and USFA (4) say monthly. The First Alert manuals (6–8) say weekly ("It is important to test this unit every week"). So do Kidde's guides (12: "TEST THE ALARM WEEKLY TO ENSURE PROPER OPERATION"; 13: "Test your alarm weekly") and Kidde's night-chirp page (10). The guide should say "at least monthly", which is the NFPA/USFA minimum, and note that manuals ask for weekly.
- **Low-battery chirp interval.** NFPA says "every 30 or 60 seconds" (1, 2). Kidde says 30–40 seconds, or 60 seconds on some alarms (9, 12, 13). First Alert manuals say about once a minute or every 43 seconds (6, 7, 8). This varies by model. "Roughly every 30 to 60 seconds" covers every source except the First Alert SM110V-AC note that early chirps can be "up to several hours" apart (8).
- **End-of-life pattern.** This varies by model. Kidde sealed models: chirp every 30 seconds (9). Kidde 20SD10 family: 2 chirps every 60 seconds plus 2 amber LED blinks (13). First Alert P1210: "3 horn pulses every 43 seconds" (6). First Alert SM210M: "5 chirps approximately every 43 seconds" (7). First Alert SM110V-AC: "5 chirps every minute" plus 5 green flashes (8). On some models a 3-chirp pattern means end of life (P1210), and on others it means a malfunction (SM210M, Kidde 20SD10). Tell readers to check the label or manual, and to replace the alarm if it keeps chirping after a fresh battery.
- **Silencing a low-battery or end-of-life chirp.** This varies by model. First Alert P1210: 8 hours (6). Kidde i12040A: about 13 hours (12). Kidde 20SD10 family: 24 hours, and no longer possible after 7 days (13). First Alert SM210M gives no duration (7). Kidde's alarm-sound page (fetched, not listed above) says "up to 12 hours" on newer units. No source supports silencing end of life for 48 hours. Kidde's own CO page says its CO end-of-life chirp "will not stop until the unit is powered off" (9).
- **Nuisance hush length.** Kidde Hush is "approximately 7 minutes" (9), and its Smart Hush and i12040A hush are up to 8 minutes (12). First Alert's is up to 9 minutes (6, 7). The NFPA 2015 installation guide (not listed above) says "roughly 7 minutes". Safe wording: "a few minutes (about 7 to 10, depending on the model)".
- **What the 10 years counts from.** NFPA (1) and USFA (4) count from the manufacture date. Kidde i12040A counts from the date code (12). Kidde 20SD10 counts from initial power-up and self-alerts (13). First Alert SM110V-AC says "10 years after installation" (8). The older First Alert P1200 manual says "from date of purchase" (fetched, not listed). Use the NFPA/USFA wording, the manufacture date printed on the back.
- **Reset hold time.** Kidde says hold the test button "for at least 15 seconds" with the battery out (11). First Alert's procedure, which is for discharging before servicing a hardwired alarm and is not described as a chirp reset, says "for 5-10 seconds" (8). No official source found gives "15–20 seconds".
- **CO alarm life (low priority).** Kidde says 7 years (9, 11), "seven to ten years" (10), and its CO page (fetched, not listed) says to replace CO alarms installed "5-7 years ago". NFPA defers to the manufacturer (1, 2).
- **Interconnected chirping.** No source states outright that only the unit with the problem chirps. First Alert's Low Battery Latch (8) is designed so the affected unit can be identified by its LED, which supports the idea. Kidde (11) warns that a loose neutral wire "can cause an echoing chirp from one unit to the next". The guide should say "usually only the alarm with the problem chirps; look for the one whose light is blinking differently", and should not present this as a universal rule.
