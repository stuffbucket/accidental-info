# Sources for An Accidental Guide to… Is My Houseplant Dying?

All fetched 22 October 2026. Excerpts are verbatim and short. Public, official sources only.

## 1. Royal Horticultural Society, "How to help a poorly houseplant"

<https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/houseplants/how-to-help-a-poorly-houseplant>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: none displayed (page metadata gives 12 March 2026).

- Common signs of trouble: "Wilting, yellowing, browning and leaf drop are common signs of poor health"
- Winter is a risk time: "Problems often arise in winter due to overwatering, poor light or dry air"
- Most plants can recover: "Many houseplants will recover quickly once their growing conditions are improved"
- Too little light: "Weak, spindly growth, pale leaves and poor flowering are all signs that a plant isn’t getting enough light."
- Too little light, more signs: "You might also see lower leaves turning yellow and falling."
- Loss of variegation: "plants kept in a shady spot might revert to green."
- Winter light (UK, northern hemisphere): "over winter, when sunlight is weaker, many houseplants benefit from being moved closer to south- or west-facing windows."
- Too much light: "Strong, direct sunlight, such as from a south-facing window in summer, can cause brown scorched patches on leaves and give them a washed-out appearance."
- Thirsty signs: "Wilted leaves, brown and crispy leaf edges and flowers that fade or fall quickly are all signs that a plant isn’t getting enough water."
- Thirsty vs drowning, which leaves go first: "Unlike with overwatering (below), older leaves at the base of a dry plant will often yellow and fall first."
- Feel the soil: "Insert your finger into the growing medium to check how moist it is a few centimetres down."
- Don't water on a schedule: "Rather than watering regularly at set intervals, it is good practice to check the moisture levels and only water when necessary."
- Wilting can mean either: "Overwatering can also cause wilting, and it’s easy to mistake this for a lack of water and drown a plant, especially over winter when growth slows."
- Check for hidden water: "Lift your plant out of its pot to see if water has collected in the base, tipping away any excess."
- Drowning signs: "Other signs of overwatering include old and new leaves yellowing and dropping at the same time, browning in the middle of leaves, mouldy patches on leaves and flowers, stem rot and dark, mushy roots."
- Drowning can kill: "Regular overwatering can lead to oedema and whole plant collapse."
- Humidity (low priority): "Brown leaf tips and edges are a classic sign of low air humidity"
- Cutting back: "Cut off any mouldy, shrivelled, completely brown or completely yellow leaves with secateurs, as these won’t recover."
- Not stated: a scratch test; how to judge whether a whole plant is dead; healthy root colour; fungus gnats (not on this page); mould on the soil surface (mould is mentioned on "leaves and flowers" only).

## 2. Royal Horticultural Society, "How to grow houseplants" (RHS Growing Guide)

<https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/houseplants/growing-guide>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: none displayed (page metadata gives 19 August 2026).

- Wilting in wet compost points to drowning (in the buying-a-plant advice): "Especially avoid buying those wilting while in moist, or wet compost, as this can indicate root damage from overwatering, or root disease"
- Pot weight: "Drier pots feel lighter than wet ones"
- No fixed schedule: "Don’t water routinely, check that the pot needs water first"
- Temperature changes frequency: "Plants in a cool room will need watering less often than plants in a warm room"
- Water thoroughly: "Water the pot thoroughly so that water escapes from the drainage hole"
- Drainage holes: "Containers should always have a drainage hole to allow the water to escape"
- Why standing water drowns roots: "If your plants are allowed to sit in water for long periods, the airspaces in the soil can be replaced with water and cause the roots to drown"
- Empty the saucer: "Once your plants have been watered, allow them to drain thoroughly and then empty any water-filled saucers"
- Winter watering: "In winter, houseplants can be left for a week or two without water as long as the room temperature is cool, but does not fall below the minimum tolerated"
- Winter light: "In winter, houseplants will benefit from full sunlight when daylight is weak"
- Too much light: "In summer strong sunlight can scorch susceptible plants through glass"
- Quick symptom list: "Brown leaf edges – possible under-watering"
- Quick symptom list: "Plant collapse, or yellowing leaves which drop – possible overwatering"
- Fungus gnats (low priority): "Sciarid flies (fungus gnats) – look for minute black flies"
- Feeding (low priority): "Feeding during the winter should be limited to once a month"
- Humidity (low priority): "Spraying can be helpful if done frequently"
- Not stated: a specific depth to test the soil (the page says to water when "the surface of the compost becomes dry", with a glossary pop-up inside that sentence on the page); root rot appearance; whether a plant is dead.
- Note: this page also says "Pots dry out faster in winter when the atmospheric humidity is lowered by central heating" (with a glossary pop-up inside the sentence). See "Where sources disagree".

## 3. University of Maryland Extension (Home & Garden Information Center), "Overwatered Indoor Plants"

<https://extension.umd.edu/resource/overwatered-indoor-plants>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: Updated March 13, 2023.

- Why too much water hurts: "Excess water reduces oxygen in the soil, which damages fine roots and renders the plant unable to take up water."
- Drowning looks like drought: "Plants exposed to excess moisture show the same symptoms as plants with root rots, crown rots (crown rot from overwatering) or drought stress."
- Main drowning sign: "The primary symptom of excess moisture is wilting or yellowing of lower and inner leaves."
- It can kill: "If excess water continues, plants may show other drought symptoms, such as scorch, leaf drop, and/ or plant death."
- Pot weight: "As plants use water, the potting mix will dry out and become lighter in weight."
- Water thoroughly: "Water plants thoroughly so that water comes out of the bottom of the pot."
- Soil pulling away (thirsty): "Plants that have dried to the point that the media has pulled away from the sides of the pot may need several applications of water to rehydrate the potting mix."
- Saucers and fungus gnats: "Pour off any water that collects in saucers under the pots to prevent problems with fungus gnats and other insects."
- Not stated: root appearance; light; winter frequency.

## 4. University of Maryland Extension (Home & Garden Information Center), "Diagnose Indoor Plant Problems"

<https://extension.umd.edu/resource/diagnose-indoor-plant-problems>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: Updated: February 5, 2025.

- Wilting has two causes: "Overwatering or lack of water: overwatering can cause root rots."
- Healthy roots: "Healthy roots should be light-colored and firm."
- Drainage: "Water should be able to drain from the bottom of containers."
- Rotten roots: "Diseased roots will appear dark and soft."
- Rescue after root rot (cut back and repot): "Remove diseased plants from their pots, cut out portions (rotten), and replant remaining healthy sections."
- Rescue by propagation: "Take cuttings and root them in sterile potting mix."
- When to give up: "Severely damaged plants should be discarded."
- Too much light: "Excessive light or sunburn."
- Too little light (spindly growth): "Increase light levels by relocating plants closer to a light source or add additional artificial light sources."
- Fungus gnats: "Fungus gnats: tiny black flies flying near plants or near light sources."
- Fungus gnat remedy: "Allow potting media to dry between waterings."
- Pests (low priority): "Regularly rinse plants with water to keep down pest problems."
- Not stated: smell of rotten roots; scratch test; how often to water.

## 5. University of Maryland Extension (Home & Garden Information Center), "Lighting for Indoor Plants"

<https://extension.umd.edu/resource/lighting-indoor-plants>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: Updated: March 10, 2023.

- Light matters most: "Light is probably the most essential factor for healthy indoor plant growth."
- Light falls off with distance: "Light intensity depends upon the distance of the light source from the plant and decreases rapidly with increasing distance."
- Season affects light: "Factors like the presence of curtains, insect screening, weather, seasons of the year, shade from trees and other buildings, and the cleanliness and type of window all impact light intensity."
- Too much light: "When a plant gets too much direct light, the leaves become pale, turn brown, and die."
- Too little light, leggy and leaning: "Indoor plants can become spindly or “leggy”, as they stretch to reach for more light. When grown where light reaches them from one direction, they can develop a lean."
- Too little light, more signs: "Additional symptoms can include a fading of leaf color, diminished flowering, and poor growth."
- Window direction (table): low light is "North facing windows"; medium-bright is "East or west-facing windows"; high light is "South-facing windows or some west-facing windows".
- Failure from poor light: "Houseplants can fail after a healthy start because of inadequate light."
- Leaf drop after a move is adjustment: "Moving indoor plants back inside after spending the summer outside can cause leaf drop and yellowing."
- Adjustment takes time: "It takes time for plants to adjust to lower light conditions inside homes."
- Shock at purchase: "Plants can already be stressed by the switch from growing in a greenhouse to awaiting sale in a store that may not be well-lit."
- Not stated: that most problems are light problems; loss of variegation; small leaves (it says brighter-grown plants "have normal size leaves").

## 6. Clemson Cooperative Extension Home & Garden Information Center, "Indoor Plants – Watering" (HGIC 1459)

<https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: Reviewed: Jun 21, 2024 (originally published 09/99).

- Overwatering kills: "The main cause of death of potted plants is over-watering."
- Why: "Roots need both water and oxygen, and when surrounded by water, they cannot take up oxygen."
- Thirst and drowning look alike: "The symptoms of over-watering and underwatering are similar."
- Frequency depends: "The amount and frequency of watering depends on many factors, such as the plant species, its growth stage, its location, the type and size of its pot, soil mix characteristics and variable weather conditions."
- Light and warmth change frequency: "A plant in a warm, dry, sunny location needs more frequent watering than one in a cool, low-light environment."
- Resting plants need less: "During these periods and while it is dormant, a plant will need less water."
- Feel the soil: "Stick your finger into the mix up to the first joint; if it is dry at your fingertip it needs water."
- Soil shrinking from the pot: "When the potting mix in a clay pot begins to dry, it shrinks away from the sides of the pot."
- Pot weight: "As potting mixtures become dry, a definite loss in weight can be observed."
- Water thoroughly, empty the saucer: "Apply water until it runs out of the bottom of the pot." / "Don’t let the pot sit in the water that runs out. Empty the saucer."
- Wilting can mean either: "Although wilting is often an indication of the need to water, it is not always so."
- Watering a rotting plant makes it worse: "This inability to take up water will cause wilting, and under these conditions, watering may make the problem worse."
- Not stated: light symptoms; root colour; whether a plant is dead.

## 7. Iowa State University Extension and Outreach (Yard and Garden), "How to Care for Houseplants"

<https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/how-care-houseplants>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: Last reviewed: October 2023.

- Light is the main limit: "For most indoor gardeners, light is the most limiting environmental factor for growing houseplants, becoming one of the most important considerations."
- Light falls off with distance: "Light levels drop quickly as you get further away from the window."
- How far: "Often, areas more than a few feet from even bright windows will have light intensities that are too low to successfully grow houseplants."
- Window direction: "South or east-facing windows often provide the most abundant light."
- Too little light: "if leaves drop, flowers fail to develop, or growth is pale, spindly, or slow, then light levels may be too low."
- Too much light: "If leaves are bleached or damaged, or if they turn yellow or brown and drop, light levels may be too high."
- Watering is the top problem: "Improper watering is the number one reason people have problems with houseplants."
- Schedules overwater: "It is very easy to overwater houseplants."
- Season changes frequency: "watering frequency changes depending on factors like light levels, temperature, humidity, type of soil and container, plant size, species, and time of year."
- Feel the soil: "If the soil is dry to the touch down an inch or so, it is time to water."
- Water thoroughly: "When water is needed, water thoroughly until water drains out the bottom."
- Empty the saucer: "Always remove any excess water in the saucer or outer pot after about 30 to 45 minutes."
- Standing water rots roots: "Never allow plants to sit in water. Roots that stay in water for long periods will rot."
- Drainage holes: "All containers must have a drainage hole. Without one, the soil stays wet and roots rot and die."
- Gravel is not a substitute: "Putting a layer of gravel in the bottom does not make up for the lack of a drainage hole."
- Cachepots: "Double pots (or cache pots) use the desirable container as an outer “sleeve” for a slightly smaller container with a drainage hole to set down inside."
- Repotting (remove bad roots): "Loosen the root mass, lightly break up the soil, and remove any limp, brown, or dead roots."
- Humidity (low priority): "This low humidity can lead to slow growth, dried-up flower buds, and brown leaf tips and edges."
- Misting (low priority): "Misting is often considered a good way to raise humidity and keep plants healthy, but in reality, it does not raise the relative humidity around the houseplant."
- Fertilizer (low priority): "Fertilize only when houseplants are actively growing."
- Not stated: root rot appearance; whether a plant is dead; loss of variegation.

## 8. University of Missouri Extension, "Lighting Indoor Houseplants" (G6515)

<https://extension.missouri.edu/publications/g6515>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: Revised (page lists June 2016 and September 2017).

- Light is the main limit: "Lack of adequate light is the most common factor limiting the growth of plants in many areas of the home."
- Too little light: "The signs that a healthy plant is not getting enough light include development of long internodes (length of stem between leaves) or of smaller-than-normal leaves; pale green stems and foliage; and lower leaves that are yellow and dropping."
- Distance from window: "even low-light plants usually do not receive enough light at distances greater than 10 feet from an average window."
- South is brightest (US, northern hemisphere): "Windows facing south provide the brightest light conditions for the longest duration."
- Winter: "In winter, any houseplant benefits from the light of a south window."
- Too much light: "plants that do not need bright light may be sunburned by the bright light at south windows in late spring, summer or early fall."
- Other windows: "East and west windows are well suited to many plants in the medium light range, whereas north windows are satisfactory only for plants requiring the lower light levels."
- Not stated: watering advice beyond individual plant notes; loss of variegation.

## 9. University of Florida Emerging Pathogens Institute, "Diagnosing houseplants 101: Is your plant diseased or just overwatered?"

<https://epi.ufl.edu/2024/07/03/diagnosing-houseplants-101-is-your-plant-diseased-or-just-overwatered/>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: July 3, 2024. (News feature by Jiayu Liang quoting Carrie Harmon, UF/IFAS extension specialist and director of the UF/IFAS Plant Diagnostic Center. It is a UF news story, not a UF/IFAS extension fact sheet.)

- Overwatering is the top killer: "“Overwatering is actually the number one way we kill houseplants. The number two way is forgetting to water,” Harmon said."
- Check the soil, then the roots: "“If the soil is dry and crumbly, water the poor thing,” Harmon said. “If it’s wet and soggy, gently pull the plant out of the pot and look and see if the roots are white, or if they’re brown or black.”"
- Healthy vs rotten roots: "Healthy roots should be white and firm. Overwatering can cause root rot, which makes the roots turn soft and become brown, or even black."
- Smell: "The soil may also develop a sour, stinky smell as the oxygen-less soil conditions encourage anaerobic bacteria to grow."
- Cachepot trap: "That outer pot can hold onto water where the plant owner can’t see it."
- Feel the soil: "When you poke about an inch into the edge of the soil, your finger should come out just slightly dusty, with some moisture."
- Most problems are care, not disease: "Common issues include overwatering, underwatering, not providing enough light or too much light."
- Not stated: light symptoms in detail; scratch test.

## 10. N.C. Cooperative Extension, Beaufort County Center (NC State), "Should I Replace Plants That Died in the 2026 Drought?"

<https://beaufort.ces.ncsu.edu/news/should-i-replace-plants-that-died-in-the-2026-drought/>
Checked: 22 October 2026. Page date: September 24, 2026. (Written by Angela Mason Foster, Extension Master Gardener volunteer. About outdoor shrubs and perennials, not houseplants; use only for the general is-it-dead checks.)

- Looks dead is not always dead: "A plant that looks dead in August may simply be dormant, defoliated, or sacrificing its upper growth to preserve its roots."
- Scratch test: "For shrubs and other woody plants, the scratch test is one of the simplest ways to check for living tissue."
- What alive looks like: "Living tissue beneath the bark will usually appear green, pale green, or cream-colored and feel slightly moist. Brown, tan, brittle tissue indicates that portion of the stem is dead."
- Work down the stem: "If the outer twigs are dead but green tissue appears farther down, the plant may recover after the dead portions are pruned away."
- Crown check: "A firm crown with white, cream, or pale green tissue may still be alive. A crown that is soft, hollow, blackened, or foul-smelling is unlikely to recover."
- Roots: "Healthy roots are typically firm and light-colored inside. Dead roots are often brittle, hollow, dark, or mushy."
- Not stated: anything specific to houseplants or indoor conditions.

## Where sources disagree

- Which window is brightest. Missouri says "Windows facing south provide the brightest light conditions for the longest duration", and UMD's table puts south-facing in the high-light row. Iowa State says "South or east-facing windows often provide the most abundant light" and adds that south and east are "not universally better". The RHS (UK) recommends moving plants "closer to south- or west-facing windows" in winter. All of these are northern-hemisphere sources.
- How far from a window is too far. Iowa State says "more than a few feet"; Missouri says "greater than 10 feet from an average window" (for low-light plants). Both agree light drops quickly with distance.
- How dry before watering. Clemson says dry "up to the first joint". Iowa State says "an inch or so" but also that "Ideally, the potting soil is allowed to dry out completely between waterings but the plant is not allowed to wilt." UF says the finger should come out "just slightly dusty, with some moisture", and that completely dry, dusty soil means water now. The RHS says "a few centimetres down". A safe common line is: water when the top inch or so (a few centimetres) is dry.
- Winter watering. The RHS growing guide says plants can go "a week or two without water" in a cool room in winter, and the "poorly houseplant" page warns about overwatering "especially over winter when growth slows". The same growing guide also says "Pots dry out faster in winter when the atmospheric humidity is lowered by central heating". So watering less in winter is right for cool rooms and slow growth, but heated rooms can dry pots out. Checking the soil covers both.
- Is overwatering or light the top problem? Clemson ("main cause of death"), UF ("number one way we kill houseplants") and Iowa State ("Improper watering is the number one reason") put watering first. Missouri and Iowa State call light the "most limiting" or "most common factor limiting the growth", and UMD calls it "probably the most essential factor". No source read says that most houseplant problems relate to light. The guide should say light is the factor that most often limits growth, not that it causes most problems.
- Misting. The RHS says "Spraying can be helpful if done frequently"; Iowa State says misting "does not raise the relative humidity around the houseplant".

## Gaps (no source read supports these)

- Mould on the soil surface as a sign of overwatering. The RHS mentions "mouldy patches on leaves and flowers" (overwatering) and, separately, algae on the compost surface as a sign the compost has gone sour. No source read names mould on the soil.
- Soft or mushy stems are supported only as "stem rot" (RHS) and as general mushiness from bacterial rot (UF); no page read says mushy stems on their own mean overwatering.
- Scratch test for houseplants specifically. The only source is the NC State county article about outdoor woody plants.
- New growth as proof a plant is alive: no source read says this directly. The NC State article says "The absence of top growth is not conclusive evidence of death" (outdoor perennials).
- Southern-hemisphere window directions: not covered by any source read.
