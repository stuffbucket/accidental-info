# What goes in a toast? {#why}

A toast needs three things: a quick introduction, one short story or quality and a last
line that asks everyone to raise a glass. Keep the whole thing positive and aim for two
to three minutes.

Tie what you say to the occasion. Lean toward sentiment at a wedding and toward nostalgia
when someone retires.

> | Term | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | Opening | Your name and how you know the person you’re toasting. |
> | Story | One kind story or quality that everyone can follow. |
> | Last line | An invitation to raise a glass and drink. |
> | Length | Two to three minutes from start to finish. |

# How does a toast go? {#fit}

Toasts usually come once drinks are poured, at the start of the meal or just before
dessert. A toast then follows six small steps.

> | Step | What to do |
> |---|---|
> | Get attention | Stand, lift your glass or wait quietly until people notice. Don’t bang on a glass. |
> | Stand and lift your glass | Stand to signal the start. At a small, informal meal, you can stay seated. |
> | Opening | Say your name and how you know the person you’re toasting. |
> | Story | Tell one short, kind story or describe one positive quality. |
> | Last line | Raise your glass and invite everyone to join you: “To Sam.” |
> | Everyone drinks | Everyone except the person being toasted takes a sip. |

> [!TIP]
> Write a few points on a notecard, then practise the toast out loud. You’ll hear where
> a sentence is too long before the room does.

# Opening {#entry num=01 mono=Op}

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | The first sentence after the room goes quiet. |
| What | The opening is the first thing you say. Give your name and explain how you know the person you’re toasting. |
| So what | Guests who don’t know you can place you straight away. That makes the story easier to follow. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Rule of thumb | Name and connection |
> | Watch for | Starting the story too soon |

## Confused with: Getting attention

Getting attention comes first and needs no words. The opening begins when you start
speaking.

# Story {#entry num=02 mono=Sy}

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | The one memory or quality you share. |
| What | The story is one short memory or one positive quality. Pick something the whole room can follow. |
| So what | One main idea keeps the toast easy to follow. A kind story keeps the attention on the person you’re toasting. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Rule of thumb | One short, positive idea |
> | Watch for | Inside jokes and private details |

## Confused with: Roast

A roast teases someone for a laugh. A toast celebrates them, so a touch of clean humour
is fine, but leave out anything embarrassing or too personal.

# Last line {#entry num=03 mono=Lt}

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | Your final words with your glass raised. |
| What | The last line invites everyone to join the toast. Raise your glass and say something short, such as “To Sam.” |
| So what | A clear last line tells the room when to drink. Everyone else raises a glass together. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Rule of thumb | End with the invitation |
> | Watch for | Forgetting to raise your glass |

## Confused with: Reply

Your last line invites everyone to drink. The reply belongs to the person being toasted,
who smiles, nods or says thank you without drinking to themselves.

# Length {#entry num=04 mono=Ln}

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | The time from your first word to the raised glass. |
| What | Length is how long you speak. Two to three minutes is the aim for a toast you prepare. |
| So what | A short toast keeps the attention on the person you’re toasting. Practise out loud to check the time. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Rule of thumb | Two to three minutes |
> | Watch for | More than one main idea |

## Confused with: Casual toast

At a casual dinner, a few heartfelt sentences are enough, ending with something like
“To our hosts.” Two to three minutes suits a toast you prepare.

# What do I leave out? {#apart}

> | Leave out | Why |
> |---|---|
> | Inside jokes | Most of the room won’t understand them. |
> | Embarrassing stories | Off-limits. The spotlight belongs on the person you’re toasting. |
> | Overly personal details | A toast isn’t the time to overshare. |
> | Rambling | More than one main idea makes the toast hard to follow. |

# Common questions {#other}

> | Question | Answer |
> |---|---|
> | Who goes first? | The host may give the first toast, at a dinner party or a wedding. If the host doesn’t, a guest may toast the hosts. |
> | Do I stand? | Stand when you give a toast. You can stay seated at a small, informal meal. |
> | Do I need a drink? | You don’t need alcohol. Raise whatever you’re drinking. A sip is enough. |
> | What does the person being toasted do? | The person being toasted stays seated and doesn’t drink. A smile, nod or thank you is enough. |
