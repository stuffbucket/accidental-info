## Wrong

### W1. Who raises a glass (line 91, Last line entry)

**Guide:** "A clear last line tells the room when to drink. Everyone raises a glass together."

**Source** (Emily Post Institute, All About Toasting, https://emilypost.com/advice/all-about-toasting):
"At the conclusion of the toast, everyone except the honoree(s) raises their glasses and
drinks."

**Verdict:** wrong. The person being toasted doesn’t raise a glass. The guide says so
itself on lines 39 and 141, so this line also contradicts the guide.

**Corrected:** "A clear last line tells the room when to drink. The guests raise their glasses together."

## Overreach

### O1. Who goes first (line 138, Common questions)

**Guide:** "At a dinner party, the host gives the first toast. If the host doesn’t, a guest may toast the hosts."

**Source** (Emily Post Institute, All About Toasting, https://emilypost.com/advice/all-about-toasting):
"At a dinner party, it’s the host or hostess’s prerogative to give the first toast." and
"The host toasts first rule does still apply at wedding receptions and other large functions."

**Verdict:** overreach. The source gives the host the right to go first, not a duty. The
guide also limits the rule to dinner parties, when the source extends it to weddings and
other large functions, the very events a reader is likely to face.

**Corrected:** "The host may toast first, at a dinner party or a wedding. If the host doesn’t, a guest may toast the hosts."

### O2. Casual toast (lines 120 to 123, Length entry)

**Guide:** "Confused with: One-line toast" / "A one-line toast can suit a casual dinner, such as “To us.” Two to three minutes gives you time for an opening and one story when you prepare in advance."

**Source** (Toastmasters International, How to Give a Toast, https://www.toastmasters.org/Resources/Public%20Speaking%20Tips/How%20to%20Give%20a%20Toast):
"Casual: Even a few heartfelt sentences will make an impact."

**Verdict:** overreach. The source says a few sentences, not one line, and gives no “To us.”
example. A guest thanking a host can use the source’s own salute to the hosts instead.

**Corrected:** "Confused with: Casual toast" / "At a casual dinner, a few heartfelt sentences are enough. A guest can end with “To our hosts.” Two to three minutes suits a toast you prepare."

### O3. The spotlight reason (lines 71 and 130)

**Guide:** line 71 "A kind story keeps the attention on the person you’re toasting." and line 130 "Off-limits. The spotlight belongs on the person you’re toasting."

**Source** (Emily Post Institute, All About Toasting, https://emilypost.com/advice/all-about-toasting):
"Keep it short and to the point. You want the spotlight to stay on the toastee, not you."
and (Toastmasters) "Remember, a toast isn’t the time to roast, embarrass, or overshare. It's
an opportunity to celebrate."

**Verdict:** overreach. The source gives the spotlight as the reason to keep a toast short,
which line 111 already uses correctly. The sources give a different reason for kind stories
and for leaving out embarrassing ones: a toast is there to celebrate.

**Corrected:** line 71 "One main idea keeps the toast focused. A positive story keeps the toast a celebration."
Line 130 "Stories that might embarrass the person are off-limits. A toast celebrates them."

### O4. What a toast needs (line 13, What goes in a toast?)

**Guide:** "A toast needs three things: a quick introduction, one short story or quality and a last line that asks everyone to raise a glass."

**Source** (Toastmasters International, How to Give a Toast, URL as O2): "It typically
includes a brief story, kind words, and a closing line where guests raise their glasses in
unison."

**Verdict:** overreach, low. The source says “typically includes”, not “needs”. The same
source says a casual toast can be a few sentences, so the rule is softer than the guide
makes it.

**Corrected:** "A toast usually has three parts: an opening, one short story or quality and a last line that asks everyone to raise a glass."

### O5. Last line watch-for (line 98, Last line entry)

**Guide:** "Watch for | Forgetting to raise your glass"

**Source** (Toastmasters International, How to Give a Toast, URL as O2): "Forgetting to end
with a call to join you in the toast"

**Verdict:** overreach, low. The source names the missing call to drink as the mistake. A
raised glass is part of ending clearly (“Close with a strong toast invitation and raise
your glass”), but the source’s listed mistake is the call.

**Corrected:** "Watch for | Ending without a call to drink"

## Unsourced

### U1. What the person being toasted does (line 141, and line 103)

**Guide:** line 141 "The person being toasted stays seated and doesn’t drink. A smile, nod or thank you is enough." Line 102 to 103 "The reply belongs to the person being toasted, who smiles, nods or says thank you without drinking to themselves."

**Source** (Emily Post Institute, All About Toasting, https://emilypost.com/advice/all-about-toasting):
"The honoree acknowledges the toast with a smile or nod."

**Verdict:** unsourced. No source says the person stays seated or says thank you. A
retiring colleague who stands and replies would be breaking a rule no source states. Either
find a public source and add it to `sources.md`, or cut the claim.

**Corrected:** line 141 "The person being toasted doesn’t raise a glass or drink. A smile or nod is enough."
Line 102 to 103 "Your last line invites everyone to drink. The person being toasted doesn’t drink and answers with a smile or nod."

### U2. Hearing long sentences before the room does (line 43, Tip)

**Guide:** "You’ll hear where a sentence is too long before the room does."

**Verdict:** unsourced. The source says to rehearse out loud but gives no reason. It does
give a related, useful rule: "don’t include words you wouldn’t use in your everyday life."
Either find a public source and add it to `sources.md`, or cut the claim.

**Corrected:** "Use only words you’d use in everyday life."

### U3. What a roast is (line 82, Story entry)

**Guide:** "A roast teases someone for a laugh. A toast celebrates them, so leave out anything embarrassing or too personal."

**Source** (Toastmasters, URL as O2): "Remember, a toast isn’t the time to roast, embarrass,
or overshare. It's an opportunity to celebrate." (Emily Post, All About Toasting): "A touch
of humor is acceptable, but keep it clean; stories that might embarrass the honoree are
off-limits."

**Verdict:** unsourced, low. No source defines a roast. The bigger problem is what the
paragraph implies: that a toast has no humour. Both Emily Post pages allow a touch of clean
humour. Either find a public source for the definition, or rewrite from the source.

**Corrected:** "A toast isn’t the time to roast or embarrass anyone. A touch of clean humour is fine. Leave out anything embarrassing or too personal."

### U4. Opening watch-for (line 58, Opening entry)

**Guide:** "Watch for | Starting the story too soon"

**Verdict:** unsourced, low. No source names this as a mistake. The nearest is "Speaking
without structure". The source’s instruction is to say who you are and how you know the
person, so the mistake to watch for is skipping that.

**Corrected:** "Watch for | Skipping how you know them"

## Naming

### N1. “Reply” (line 100, Last line entry)

**Guide:** "Confused with: Reply"

**Source** (Emily Post Institute, All About Toasting): "The honoree acknowledges the toast
with a smile or nod."

**Verdict:** naming. The source says the person acknowledges the toast. “Reply” suggests a
spoken answer or a toast in return, which no source describes.

**Corrected:** "Confused with: Acknowledging a toast"

### N2. “Private details” (line 78, Story entry)

**Guide:** "Watch for | Inside jokes and private details"

**Source** (Toastmasters, URL as O2): "skip any inside jokes or overly personal details."

**Verdict:** naming, low. The source term is “overly personal details”, which line 131 also
uses. Line 83 says “too personal”. One name helps the reader match the Story entry to the
leave-out table.

**Corrected:** "Watch for | Inside jokes and overly personal details"

## Advice in the sources that the guide leaves out

Not a verdict, but each gap matters to a reader. The reader panel gives rewrites.

- Tie the toast to the occasion, with sentiment at a wedding and nostalgia for a retiring
  employee (Emily Post, All About Toasting).
- A touch of clean humour is acceptable (Emily Post, All About Toasting).
- When in the meal: "usually at the beginning of the meal, or just before dessert" (same).
- How to get attention: "Stand, raise a glass or hold a meaningful silence" (Emily Post,
  Tips You Can Use Today).
- "Even a few heartfelt sentences will make an impact." (Toastmasters).
