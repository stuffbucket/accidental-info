## Who it's for

Someone who has just been asked to "say a few words" at a wedding, a birthday dinner, a
leaving do or a family meal. They aren't a speaker and they're dreading it. The author
wants a calm, plain read that makes a toast feel small and doable, with a light, dry, warm
touch at most: one line of understatement in the whole guide is plenty, and it must also
say something useful. No jokes with a set-up, no exclamation marks, no snark. No sample
toasts with made-up names beyond a short "To Sam." as an example of a last line.

## The question, and the answer in order

How do I give a toast?

1. A toast is short: who you are, one short story, and a raised glass. Two to three minutes.
2. How it goes, step by step (the `{#fit}` table).
3. Opening: your name and how you know the person you're toasting.
4. Story: one short story or one quality, positive, that everyone in the room can follow.
   One main idea.
5. Last line: raise your glass and invite everyone to join, such as "To Sam."
6. Length: two to three minutes. Write it on a notecard and practise out loud.
7. What to leave out, and common questions (who goes first, standing, drinks, what the
   person being toasted does).

## Length

About 800 words in guide.md, sources included, which prints as about 900. Rough budget: why
120, fit 120, each entry 85, leave-out table 70, common questions 80. Cut detail, not
clarity. Short, but not staccato.

## What goes where

Front matter: replace PLACEHOLDER in `subtitle` so it reads "A pocket-sized guide to <four
to eight words>." Change nothing else.

`{#why}`: paragraph 1 (about 40 words) answers the question and stands alone (the website
shows it by itself). Paragraph 2 (about 30 words) leads into the four terms. Terms table:
one plain defining sentence per term, under 14 words.

`{#fit}`: one or two sentences, then the steps table. Keep the Step labels exactly. Each
What-to-do cell is one short sentence or two:

- Get attention: wait for drinks to be poured, then signal gently and wait for the room to
  settle. Don't bang on a glass.
- Stand and lift your glass: standing signals the start (at a small, informal meal you can
  stay seated).
- Opening: your name and how you know them.
- Story: one short, kind story or quality.
- Last line: raise your glass and invite everyone: "To Sam."
- Everyone drinks: everyone except the person being toasted takes a sip.

The callout: one practical tip, such as writing the toast as a few points on a notecard and
practising it out loud. One or two sentences.

Entries: Identify by, What, So what, then two field marks (Rule of thumb, Watch for) as
short answers of three to six words with no full stop, then Confused with in two flat
sentences. About 85 words each. Keep entries short: two entries share each page.

- What: the first sentence is a definition that reads alone in bold ("The opening is …").
  Then what it does.
- So what: the first sentence says why it matters, readable alone in bold.
- No sentence in a row starts with "It", "Its", "They" or "This".

Confused with (each difference is in `sources.md`):
- Opening / Getting attention: getting attention comes first and needs no words: stand,
  raise a glass or wait for the room to settle. The opening is your first words.
- Story / Roast: a roast teases. A toast celebrates, so it doesn't roast, embarrass or
  overshare.
- Last line / Reply: your last line invites everyone to drink. The reply belongs to the
  person being toasted, who smiles, nods or says thank you, and doesn't drink to themselves.
- Length / One-line toast: at a casual dinner a toast can be one line, such as "To us." Two
  to three minutes is for a toast you prepare.

`{#apart}` What do I leave out? One short reason per row (from `sources.md`): inside jokes
(most of the room won't understand them), embarrassing stories (off-limits, and the
spotlight should stay on the person you're toasting), overly personal details (a toast
isn't the time to overshare), rambling (stick to one main idea).

`{#other}` Common questions, one or two sentences each: the host gives the first toast at a
dinner party, and a guest may toast the hosts if they don't; the person giving the toast
stands, except at a small, informal meal; you don't need alcohol, and a sip will do; the
person being toasted stays seated, doesn't drink, and smiles, nods or says thank you.

Leave `{#sources}` exactly as it is.

## How to write it

- Regular English, normal word order, "you" for the reader.
- Build one idea at a time. No paragraph by list. Lists go in tables.
- No names of people except the placeholder "Sam" in an example last line. No quotes from
  the sources.
- Say "the person you’re toasting", not "honoree" or "toastee".
- Typographic apostrophes and quotes (’ “ ”). No em dashes, semicolons, exclamation marks,
  "&", slashes in words, backticks or emphasis.
- Never write about the guide itself: no "this guide", "below", "this page".
