# Giving a Toast: reader panel

Reviewed: `drafts/giving-a-toast/guide.md`, version 0.2.0, and its 7-page PDF (page images
and `guide.txt`), read 22 October 2026. Each page was read as three readers with no access
to the sources. Rewrites were then checked against `notes/sources.md` so that each uses only
sourced facts.

- **Shy friend.** Asked to toast at a friend’s wedding. Dreads speaking, wants to know
  exactly what to say and what not to do.
- **Manager.** Has to toast a retiring colleague at a work event. Short on time, knows
  everyone in the room, may be the host.
- **Dinner guest.** At a casual dinner, wants to raise a glass to thank the host. Needs to
  know whether a short thank-you counts and when to do it.

The voice pass checks the guide against the review skill’s `checklist.md`.

## Top ten fixes

Most serious first.

**1. The guide implies a toast can’t be funny.**
Page 4, line 82 to 83: "A roast teases someone for a laugh. A toast celebrates them, so
leave out anything embarrassing or too personal." With “Stay positive” and the leave-out
table, the shy friend reads this as “no jokes”, and a wedding toast without one light line
feels wrong to them. The sources allow a touch of clean humour.
Rewrite line 82 to 83: "A toast isn’t the time to roast or embarrass anyone. A touch of clean humour is fine. Leave out anything embarrassing or too personal."

**2. Nothing says how to fit the toast to the occasion.**
Page 2, lines 17 to 18: "The opening tells the room who you are. The story gives them one
reason to celebrate, and the last line brings everyone in." This repeats line 13. The shy
friend and the manager both want to know what kind of story to pick, and the sources answer
it directly.
Rewrite lines 17 to 18: "Tie what you say to the occasion. At a wedding, lean toward sentiment. For a retirement, lean toward nostalgia."

**3. The manager doesn’t believe the retiree “stays seated”.**
Page 6, line 141: "The person being toasted stays seated and doesn’t drink. A smile, nod or
thank you is enough." At a leaving do the person often stands to reply. No source says they
stay seated, so the manager has reason to doubt it and then doubts the rest of the table.
Rewrite line 141: "The person being toasted doesn’t raise a glass or drink. A smile or nod is enough."

**4. “Who goes first?” covers only dinner parties.**
Page 6, line 138: "At a dinner party, the host gives the first toast. If the host doesn’t,
a guest may toast the hosts." The shy friend asks “and at a wedding?” The manager asks “am I
the host?” The sources say the host-first rule also applies at weddings and large events.
Rewrite line 138: "The host may toast first, at a dinner party or a wedding. If the host doesn’t, a guest may toast the hosts."

**5. The dinner guest’s case gets a toast to the wrong people.**
Page 5, lines 120 to 123: "A one-line toast can suit a casual dinner, such as “To us.”" The
guest wants to thank the host, and “To us.” toasts the table. The sources say a few
heartfelt sentences are enough and that a guest may salute the hosts.
Rewrite lines 120 to 123: "Confused with: Casual toast" / "At a casual dinner, a few heartfelt sentences are enough. A guest can end with “To our hosts.” Two to three minutes suits a toast you prepare."

**6. The guide doesn’t say when in the meal to toast.**
Page 3, lines 29 to 30: "Wait until drinks have been poured. A toast then follows six small
steps, from getting the room’s attention to taking a sip." The dinner guest still doesn’t
know whether to stand up at the starter or the pudding.
Rewrite lines 29 to 30: "Toast once drinks are served, usually at the start of the meal or just before dessert. A toast then follows six steps."

**7. “Signal gently” doesn’t say how.**
Page 3, line 34: "Signal gently and wait for the room to settle. Don’t bang on a glass." The
shy friend’s first worry is how to get a loud room to listen. The guide rules out one
method but gives none.
Rewrite line 34: "Stand, lift your glass or wait in silence until people notice. Don’t bang on a glass."

**8. The person being toasted is told not to drink, then told to raise a glass.**
Page 3, line 39 labels the step "Everyone drinks", and page 5, line 91 says "Everyone raises
a glass together." Both contradict line 39’s own text and line 141. The shy friend, who is
toasting the couple, wonders whether the couple should drink.
Rewrite line 39: "| Guests drink | Everyone except the person being toasted takes a sip. |"
Rewrite line 91: "A clear last line tells the room when to drink. The guests raise their glasses together."

**9. Two Identify-by and Watch-for lines don’t parse.**
Page 4, line 58: "Starting the story too soon". Too soon after what? Line 69: "The one
memory or quality you share." Share with the room, or have in common with the person? The
shy friend rereads both and gives up.
Rewrite line 58: "Watch for | Skipping how you know them"
Rewrite line 69: "| Identify by | The one memory or quality you tell the room about. |"

**10. The tip makes the shy friend more nervous.**
Page 3, lines 42 to 43: "You’ll hear where a sentence is too long before the room does."
This pictures the room judging them. The sources give a calmer and more useful line.
Rewrite lines 42 to 43: "Write a few points on a notecard, then practise the toast out loud. Use only words you’d use in everyday life."

## What the shy friend needs and doesn’t get

## Verdict

All three readers would finish: the guide is short and the steps are clear. The shy friend
leaves knowing the shape of a toast but thinking jokes are banned and with no idea how to
pitch a wedding toast. The manager leaves doubting the advice on what the retiree does. The
dinner guest leaves without a clear “a few sentences is fine, end with ‘To our hosts’”.
Fixes 1 to 5 close all three gaps with sourced facts.
