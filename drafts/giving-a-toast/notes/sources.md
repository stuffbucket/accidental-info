# Sources for An Accidental Guide to… Giving a Toast

All read on 22 October 2026. Excerpts are verbatim and short. Public sources: the Emily
Post Institute's etiquette advice and Toastmasters International's public-speaking tips.
Sample toasts on those pages name made-up or example people; none of them go in the guide.

## 1. Structure, length, one story, what to leave out

Toastmasters International, "How to Give a Toast" (Public Speaking Tips). No page date.
<https://www.toastmasters.org/Resources/Public%20Speaking%20Tips/How%20to%20Give%20a%20Toast>

- "A toast is a heartfelt acknowledgement of a special moment, big or small. It typically includes a brief story, kind words, and a closing line where guests raise their glasses in unison."
- Quick steps: "Introduce yourself and your connection." "Share a brief, meaningful story or quality." "Highlight the person or occasion." "End with a clear toast invitation (for example, “To [Name]!”)"
- Length: "Aim for a length of two to three minutes." "Keep it short: Two to three minutes is ideal."
- Opening: "Introduce yourself and your relationship to the honoree."
- Story: "Add a short, meaningful anecdote or describe a quality that reflect the honoree’s character. Remember to keep it positive and skip any inside jokes or overly personal details."
- "Keep it focused: Stick to one main idea."
- "Stay positive: Avoid embarrassing or uncomfortable jokes/stories."
- "Remember, a toast isn’t the time to roast, embarrass, or overshare. It's an opportunity to celebrate."
- Mistakes: "Including inside jokes others won’t understand", "Sharing overly personal or sensitive stories", "Speaking without structure", "Forgetting to end with a call to join you in the toast"
- Audience: "Mixed groups: simple, inclusive, and broadly relatable"
- Preparing: "Write it down: Use notecards with bullet points." "Practice: Rehearse out loud."
- Delivering: "Get everyone’s attention: Gently signal for attention and wait for the room to settle." "Stand and lift your glass: This signals the start of your toast and draws attention." "Stay on topic: Stick to the main theme and avoid rambling." "End clearly: Close with a strong toast invitation and raise your glass."
- Casual: "Even a few heartfelt sentences will make an impact."
- Be yourself: "don’t include words you wouldn’t use in your everyday life."

## 2. Toasting etiquette: who, when, standing, drinking

Emily Post Institute, "All About Toasting". No page date.
<https://emilypost.com/advice/all-about-toasting>

- "At a dinner party, it’s the host or hostess’s prerogative to give the first toast."
- "If a host doesn’t offer a toast, a guest may propose a toast saluting the hosts."
- "Typically, toasts are proposed as soon as the wine, Champagne, or other beverage is served – usually at the beginning of the meal, or just before dessert."
- "At the conclusion of the toast, everyone except the honoree(s) raises their glasses and drinks."
- "The honoree acknowledges the toast with a smile or nod."
- "Everyone else—include the person being toasted—remains seated, unless the toaster instructs everyone to do otherwise"
- "When toasted, the “toastee” does not stand, nor drink to herself. Once the toast is finished, she simply rises, bows her acknowledgement, and says thank you."
- "No need for everyone to drain their glasses during a toast – a sip will do just fine."
- "You don’t need an alcoholic beverage to propose or drink to a toast."
- "Keep is short, positive, and to the point. A touch of humor is acceptable, but keep it clean; stories that might embarrass the honoree are off-limits."
- "The best toasts are short and come from the heart. If you’re stuck, try to tie the toast to the occasion."
- "The person delivering the toast stands (the exception: a small, informal occasion)."
- "Keep it short and to the point. You want the spotlight to stay on the toastee, not you. Tie what you say to the event that is being celebrated. Write out what you will say ahead of time and practice."
- "Lean toward sentimentality at a wedding, nostalgia for a retiring employee. A touch of humor is rarely out of place."
- "The host toasts first rule does still apply at wedding receptions and other large functions."

Emily Post Institute, "Toasts and Toasting: Tips You Can Use Today". No page date.
<https://emilypost.com/advice/toasts-and-toasting-tips-you-can-use-today>

- "Stand, raise a glass or hold a meaningful silence long enough to be noticed. Resist the urge to bang on glassware with silver or clear your throat repeatedly."
- "You can toast with with whatever you prefer to drink."

Emily Post Institute, "The Groom's Toast". No page date.
<https://emilypost.com/advice/the-grooms-toast>

- "Keep it short."
- "Tell brief personal (not too personal!) stories of experiences shared with your bride, but don’t tell embarrassing stories or go on and on and on."

## Not stated

- No source gives a speaking rate or a word count for two to three minutes. The guide
  gives only the minutes.
- No source here mentions exes, drinking before you speak, or reading from a phone. The
  guide leaves them out.

## Where sources agree

- Short (Emily Post), two to three minutes (Toastmasters).
- No embarrassing stories (both), no inside jokes (Toastmasters).
