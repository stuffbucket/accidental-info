## Who it's for

Someone who has just agreed, or been told, that Thanksgiving is at their place this year.
They've cooked dinners but never a whole turkey for a crowd. They are a little anxious. The
author wants a five-minute read that is calm and plain, with a light, dry, warm touch at
most: one line of understatement in the whole guide is plenty, and it must also say
something useful. No jokes with a set-up, no exclamation marks, no cute asides.

## The question, and the answer in order

How do I get Thanksgiving dinner on the table? US Thanksgiving is Thursday 26 November 2026.

1. Most of the dinner forgives mistakes. The turkey is the one dish with hard numbers: how
   big, how long to thaw, how long to roast and how hot it has to be inside.
2. Count back from dinner: buy, thaw, plan the oven, roast, check the temperature, rest.
3. Turkey size: about 1 pound per person. More if you want leftovers.
4. Thawing: in the fridge, about a day for every 4 to 5 pounds. Start days early.
5. Oven plan: the turkey takes the oven for hours. Bake what you can the day before, and
   bake the rest while the turkey rests.
6. Thermometer: 165 °F (74 °C) in the thigh, the wing and the thickest part of the breast.
   The roasting time is a guide. The thermometer decides.
7. Helpers: give guests whole dishes and give non-cooks the cleaning. Keep hot dishes hot,
   and put food away within two hours.

## Length

Five minutes front to back: about 1,000 words in guide.md, tables and sources included,
which prints as about 1,100. Rough budget: why 150, fit 150, each entry 100, common
questions 130. Cut detail, not clarity. Short, but not staccato.

## What goes where

Front matter: replace PLACEHOLDER in `subtitle` so it reads "A pocket-sized guide to
<about six words>, for 26 November 2026." Change nothing else in the front matter.

`{#why}`: paragraph 1 (about 50 words) answers the question and must stand on its own (the
website shows it alone). Paragraph 2 (about 40 words) says the turkey has the numbers and
leads into the five terms. The terms table: one plain sentence per term, under 14 words.

`{#fit}`: one or two sentences, then the countdown table. The When labels are fixed; keep
them exactly. Each What-to-do cell is one or two short sentences, with the numbers from
`sources.md`:

- Two weeks before: count guests, buy the turkey at about 1 pound each. Buy a fresh one
  only a day or two before instead.
- Saturday 21 November: a 16- to 20-pound frozen turkey goes into the fridge, in its
  wrapper, on a tray.
- Wednesday 25 November: pies, cranberry sauce, stuffing assembled in its dish and
  chilled. Set the table.
- Thursday, 11:15 a.m.: the worked example. Oven at 325 °F. A 16-pound unstuffed turkey
  takes about 3¾ to 4¼ hours.
- Thursday, 3:30 p.m.: thermometer reads 165 °F, turkey out, rests at least 20 minutes.
  Stuffing and rolls go into the oven.
- Thursday, 4 p.m.: carve and eat.
- Within two hours: leftovers into the fridge in shallow containers.

The callout: one practical tip the reader can act on now (for example, writing down the
turkey's weight and working out the thaw day the day you buy it). One or two sentences.

Entries: each has Identify by, What, So what, then three field marks (When, Rule of thumb,
Watch for) as short answers with no full stop, then Confused with. Keep each entry to
about 100 words.

- What: the first sentence is a definition that reads alone in bold ("Thawing is …",
  "A food thermometer is …"). Then what it does.
- So what: the first sentence is why it matters to the reader, readable alone in bold.
- Short answers: four to eight words, no full stop, no sentence starting "It", "They",
  "This" or "Its".
- Confused with: two flat sentences that tell the two apart.

Specific facts per entry (all in `sources.md`):

- Turkey size: 1 pound per person (FSIS). For leftovers, Food Network plans 2 pounds per
  adult and 1 per child. Confused with: Leftovers.
- Thawing: fridge at 40 °F or below, about 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds; 16 to 20 pounds is 4
  to 5 days. Cold water: 30 minutes per pound, change the water every 30 minutes, cook it
  right away. Never on the counter or in hot water. Take the giblets out after thawing.
  Confused with: Fresh turkey (never chilled below 26 °F, needs no thawing, buy one or two
  days before).
- Oven plan: the turkey holds most of the oven for hours, at 325 °F or higher. Write down
  every dish, its temperature and its time, and see what can't share the oven. Bake pies
  and assemble stuffing the day before. Confused with: Roasting timetable (FSIS's table of
  hours by weight, for the turkey only).
- Thermometer: 165 °F (74 °C), checked in the innermost thigh, the innermost wing and the
  thickest part of the breast. An oven-proof one can stay in from the start. The roasting
  times are approximate. Stuffing must reach 165 °F too. Confused with: Pop-up timer
  (FSIS says check with a thermometer as well).
- Helpers: give a guest a whole dish to bring, and give non-cooks the cleaning. Hot dishes
  must stay at 140 °F or above, and food goes into the fridge within two hours (one hour
  above 90 °F). Confused with: Cooks.

`{#apart}` Common questions: one or two sentences per answer, from `sources.md`:

- Can I cook it frozen? Yes. It takes at least 50 percent longer. Pull the giblets out
  partway through with tongs.
- Should I stuff the turkey? USDA doesn't recommend it. Bake the stuffing in a dish. If you
  stuff it, the middle of the stuffing has to reach 165 °F.
- Should I wash the turkey? No. Washing spreads bacteria around the kitchen.
- How long can food sit out? Two hours, or one hour above 90 °F.
- How long do leftovers keep? Up to four days in the fridge, so until the Monday after.
  Reheat to 165 °F.

Leave `{#sources}` exactly as it is.

## How to write it

- Regular English, normal word order, "you" for the reader.
- Build one idea at a time. Each sentence follows from the last.
- No paragraph by list. Lists go in tables.
- Write temperatures as 165 °F and 74 °C (a space before °), times as 11:15 a.m., and
  fractions as ¾ and ¼.
- No names of people or organisations in the body except "USDA", which may appear once
  or twice as the authority for the safety numbers. No quotes. No brand names.
- Typographic apostrophes and quotes (’ “ ”). No em dashes, no semicolons, no exclamation
  marks, no "&", no slashes in words, no backticks or emphasis.
- Never write about the guide itself: no "this guide", "below", "this page".
