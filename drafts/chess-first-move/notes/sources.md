# Sources for An Accidental Guide to… The First Move in Chess

All fetched 22 October 2026 (raw files saved outside the repo while writing). Excerpts are
verbatim and short. Public, official material from FIDE (the international chess federation)
and US Chess only.

## 1. FIDE Laws of Chess (taking effect from 1 January 2023)

FIDE Handbook, E.01 "FIDE Laws of Chess taking effect from 1 January 2023".
<https://handbook.fide.com/chapter/E012023>
Page date: "Applied from 01/01/2023". The handbook's list shows no later edition as of
22 October 2026.

The board and who moves first:

- 1.2: "The player with the light-coloured pieces (White) makes the first move, then the
  players move alternately, with the player with the dark-coloured pieces (Black) making the
  next move."
- 2.1: "The chessboard is composed of an 8 x 8 grid of 64 equal squares alternately light
  (the ‘white’ squares) and dark (the ‘black’ squares)." "The chessboard is placed between
  the players in such a way that the near corner square to the right of the player is white."
- 2.2: "White has 16 light-coloured pieces" ... king, queen, "Two white rooks", "Two white
  bishops", "Two white knights", "Eight white pawns" (and the same for Black).
- 2.4: "The eight vertical columns of squares are called ‘files’. The eight horizontal rows
  of squares are called ‘ranks’."

Moves used in the guide:

- 3.2 "The bishop may move to any square along a diagonal on which it stands."
- 3.4 "The queen may move to any square along the file, the rank or a diagonal on which it
  stands."
- 3.5 "the bishop, rook or queen may not move over any intervening pieces."
- 3.6 "The knight may move to one of the squares nearest to that on which it stands but not
  on the same rank, file or diagonal."
- 3.7.2 "on its first move the pawn may move as in 3.7.1 or alternatively it may advance two
  squares along the same file, provided that both squares are unoccupied"
- 3.8.2 Castling: "This is a move of the king and either rook of the same colour along the
  player's first rank, counting as a single move of the king and executed as follows: the
  king is transferred from its original square two squares towards the rook on its original
  square, then that rook is transferred to the square the king has just crossed."
- 3.8.2.1 "The right to castle has been lost: 1) If the king has already moved, or 2) With a
  rook that has already moved."
- 3.8.2.2 "Castling is prevented temporarily: 3) If the square on which the king stands, or
  the square which it must cross, or the square which it is to occupy, is attacked by one or
  more of the opponent's pieces, or 4) If there is any piece between the king and the rook"
- 4.4.1 "touches his/her king and a rook he/she must castle on that side if it is legal to
  do so"

Notation, Appendix C:

- C.2 "K=king, Q=queen, R=rook, B=bishop, N=knight. (N is used for a knight, in order to
  avoid ambiguity.)"
- C.4 "Pawns are not indicated by their first letter, but are recognised by the absence of
  such a letter. Examples: the moves are written e5, d4, a5"
- C.5 "The eight files (from left to right for White and from right to left for Black) are
  indicated by the small letters, a, b, c, d, e, f, g and h, respectively."
- C.6 "The eight ranks (from bottom to top for White and from top to bottom for Black) are
  numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8" ... "in the initial position the white pieces and pawns
  are placed on the first and second ranks; the black pieces and pawns on the eighth and
  seventh ranks."
- C.7 "each of the sixty-four squares is invariably indicated by a unique combination of a
  letter and a number."
- C.8 "Each move of a piece is indicated by the abbreviation of the name of the piece in
  question and the square of arrival." "Examples: Be5, Nf3, Rd1." "In the case of pawns,
  only the square of arrival is indicated."
- C.9 "When a piece makes a capture, an x may be inserted"
- C.13 "0-0 = castling with rook h1 or rook h8 (kingside castling)", "0-0-0 = castling with
  rook a1 or rook a8 (queenside castling)", "+ = check", "++ or # = checkmate".
- Sample game begins "1.e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nf6 3. d4" (move numbers, White's move then Black's).

## 2. FIDE, Curriculum: Chess for Refugees

FIDE (World Chess Federation), "Curriculum Chess for Refugees" (PDF created 9 October 2023).
<https://www.fide.com/docs/presentations/Curriculum%20Chess%20for%20Refugees.pdf>

Lesson fifteen, Center and development of pieces:

- "Concrete opening variations are overrated for beginners." "it is more important that they
  learn good game layout through knowledge of basic opening principles."
- Task of the opening: "developing your pieces quickly and bringing your king into safety"
- "Why to develop quickly? Because your opponent does the same."
- The six principles: "1. First develop the pawns in the center, then knights and bishops.
  2. Fight for the center! The center squares are d4, d5, e4 and e5. 3. Castle early.
  4. Move every piece only once. 5. Don't bring the queen too early into the game.
  6. Don't make too many pawns moves."
- "A lead in development means that you have more pieces taking part in the game than your
  opponent."

Lesson twelve, Castling:

- "You solve two problems in one step: bringing the king to safety and developing a rook from
  corner to a more central line."
- Queen developed last: "Queen: might be attacked by developing pieces of opponent"
- "Rooks: difficult to develop, no open lines yet to make them effective"
- "The center is usually the part of the board that opens up first. Since both players are
  fighting for control of the center, the center pawns often get exchanged – which opens up
  lines in the middle of the board."

Lesson eighteen, Scholar's Mate:

- "It ends the game after only four moves by attacking the weak f-pawn with a bishop and a
  queen. The f7-pawn is considered weak because it is defended only by the king"
- "The Scholar's Mate is common among beginners"

## 3. US Chess, Guide to Scholastic Chess (11th edition)

US Chess Federation, "Guide to Scholastic Chess", 11th edition (file dated 29 June 2021).
<https://new.uschess.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/11th-edition-guide-to-scholastic-chess-6-29-21.pdf>

- General opening theory, as taught to beginners: "A. Importance of controlling the center.
  B. Avoid moving the same piece over and over again. C. Don't bring out the queen too early.
  D. Castle early in the game. E. Don't make too many pawn moves early in the game.
  F. Develop knights and bishops quickly. G. Don't make pointless moves just to put your
  opponent in check."
- The centre: "the four squares in the center of the board: d4, d5, e4 and e5."
- "Sample Game: 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Bc5? 3.Qh5 Nf6?? 4.Qxf7# (known as Scholars Mate)"
- Algebraic notation restated from FIDE: "Each piece is indicated by the first letter, a
  capital letter, of its name."

## 4. Opening lines for the two reference pages (Wikipedia, ECO, Lichess)

Fetched 22 October 2026 as raw wikitext (`?action=raw`) and from lichess.org opening pages.
ECO codes are from Wikipedia's "List of ECO codes" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ECO_codes,
citing Matanović and Veličković 2010) unless the opening's own article gives one. Lichess
statistics are lichess.org's default filter (all speeds, ratings 1000 to 2500). The Lichess
explorer API (explorer.lichess.ovh) needs a login, and lichess.org opening pages deeper than
about four plies need one too, so the deeper lines rest on Wikipedia. The moves are played
out by packages/chess/chessdiagram.py `play()`, which rejects an illegal or ambiguous move.

"Theory likes these" means sound lines that the sources call the main or most common line.
"Theory frowns on these" means lines the sources call weak, dubious, unsound or refuted.

### White, sound

1. Ruy Lopez, Morphy Defence, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 4.Ba4 Nf6, C77.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruy_Lopez: "After 4.Ba4, Black's usual response is 4...Nf6".
   "The Morphy Defence (3...a6) is Black's most popular reply." "long been regarded as the
   most important opening among the Open Games at master level." The idea, from the article:
   the bishop attacks the knight that defends e5. Lichess: 2...Nc6 in 64% of games after 2.Nf3.
2. Italian Game, Giuoco Piano, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6, C54.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuoco_Piano and /Italian_Game: "The main move for White is 4.c3",
   "4.c3, which remains White's most common move", "After 4...Nf6, Greco's move 5.d4 was the
   main line for centuries." c3 prepares d4. The bishop on c4 aims at f7 (as in sources 2 and
   3). Lichess, Italian Game: 3...Bc5 33%.
3. Queen's Gambit Declined, 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5 Be7, D53.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Gambit_Declined: "The most common continuation is 3.Nc3 Nf6",
   "Black's most common reply is 4...Be7". The same article: 4.cxd5 "has gradually become
   more common". en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Gambit: the Accepted is "less popular than the
   Queen's Gambit Declined". Lichess, Queen's Gambit: 2...e6 27%, the top reply. In the
   Declined, Black keeps the pawn on d5 rather than taking on c4.

### White, frowned on

4. Wayward Queen Attack (Wikipedia title: Danvers Opening), 1.e4 e5 2.Qh5 Nc6 3.Bc4 g6 4.Qf3
   Nf6, C20. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danvers_Opening: "Main position after 1.e4 e5 2.Qh5 Nc6
   3.Bc4 g6 4.Qf3 Nf6 5.Ne2". "violates a conventional opening principle by developing the
   queen too early, subjecting it to attack and loss of tempo." Also: "White is not worse,
   but also has no advantage."
5. Grob's Attack, 1.g4 d5 2.Bg2 Bxg4 3.c4 d4 4.Bxb7 Nd7, A00.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grob's_Attack: diagram "After 3.c4 d4 4.Bxb7 Nd7". "It is widely
   considered to be one of the worst possible first moves for White." "the Grob is
   classified under the code A00". (An earlier choice, 3...c6 4.cxd5 cxd5, isn't in the
   sources and was dropped.) After 1.g4 the g-pawn has left White's king side.
6. Halloween Gambit, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Nxe5 Nxe5, C47.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween_Gambit: "4.Nxe5?!", "After 4... Nxe5, White usually plays
   5. d4", "refuted by 4...Nxe5 5.d4 Nc6 6.d5 Bb4!" White gives a knight for a pawn.

### Black, sound

7. Sicilian Defence, Open, 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6, B54 (B55 in Veličković).
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicilian_Defence: "Black's most common move after 2.Nf3 is 2...d6",
   "The game usually continues 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3." "the Sicilian has become the most
   played and most analysed opening at both the club and master levels" (quoting Modern Chess
   Openings). Black's c-pawn takes White's d-pawn.
8. Caro-Kann Defence, Classical, 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5, B18.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caro–Kann_Defence: "The most common way of handling the Caro–Kann,
   the Classical Variation", "2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 (or 3.Nd2) dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5". "The Caro–Kann
   remains a popular opening in top-level chess". The light-squared bishop comes out before
   e6. Lichess: 1...c6 is 8% of replies to 1.e4, fifth (1...e6, the French, is 10%).
9. King's Indian Defence, 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6, E70.
   en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Indian_Defence: "3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6", "is a common chess
   opening". en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Pawn_Game: 1...Nf6 "is the most common" reply to
   1.d4. Black lets White build a pawn center and attacks it later.

### Black, frowned on

10. Damiano Defence, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 f6 3.Nxe5 fxe5 4.Qh5+ Ke7, C40.
    en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damiano_Defence: "Taking the knight with 3...fxe5? exposes Black to
    a deadly attack after 4.Qh5+ Ke7". "It is a weak opening that gives a large advantage for
    White after 3.Nxe5."
11. Latvian Gambit, 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 f5 3.Nxe5 Qf6 4.d4 d6, C40.
    en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_Gambit: "After the usual 3...Qf6, the traditional main line
    has been 4.d4 d6 5.Nc4 fxe4". "an aggressive but objectively dubious opening for Black".
12. Englund Gambit, 1.d4 e5 2.dxe5 Nc6 3.Nf3 Qe7 4.Bf4 Qb4+, A40.
    en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Englund_Gambit: "Most common today is 2.dxe5 Nc6 3.Nf3 Qe7 4.Bf4
    Qb4+ 5.Bd2 Qxb2". "The gambit is considered weak". The queen check sets up Qxb2.

### Popularity of the first moves

- lichess.org/opening: 1.e4 59%, 1.d4 26%, 1.Nf3 4%, 1.c4 3%. Replies to 1.e4: 1...e5 40%,
  1...c5 18%, 1...d5 11%, 1...e6 10%, 1...c6 8%.
- en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Pawn_Game: 1.e4 "is the most popular opening move in chess,
  followed by 1.d4". 1...c5 "is the most common continuation in modern practice"
  (tournament play; on Lichess 1...e5 is more common).

## Not stated in the sources

- Which first move is most popular. The guide's main pages don't claim one; the reference
  pages label lines by soundness only. It presents 1.e4 and 1.d4
  as two moves that follow the first two principles (a centre pawn, two squares forward,
  which the pawn rule allows on its first move), and 1…e5 and 1…d5 as Black's replies that
  follow the same principles. The reasons given (which lines open) follow from the move
  rules in Article 3.
