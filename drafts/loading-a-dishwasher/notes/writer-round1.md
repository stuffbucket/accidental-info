## Who it's for

Someone who shares a kitchen with a person who loads the dishwasher differently, and who has been told, more than once, that they're doing it wrong. Or who has done the telling. They want to know what actually gets dishes clean, from the people who make the machines and the detergent. The guide is meant to start friendly arguments, so state the evidence-based method plainly and fairly. Where makers disagree, say who says what and that your own manual wins. Never take a side the sources don't take.

## The question, and the answer in order

Is there a right way to load a dishwasher?

1. Mostly yes, and the makers agree on more than households do. Water sprays up from an arm under each rack, so whatever faces the spray gets clean and whatever blocks it doesn't.
2. Scrape, don't rinse (as the sources say it, with the reason they give).
3. Where things go: the rack map on the next page.
4. Each part: bottom rack, top rack, cutlery basket, spray arm, detergent.
5. The usual arguments, answered from the sources, including where makers disagree.

## The series, and the tone

The series is "An Accidental Guide to…": a five-minute read for a reluctant or anxious reader who has just had this land on them. The spirit is warm and lightly self-deprecating, with dry understatement now and then, the way a kind friend who has been there would explain it. But the author's voice rules win every time: plain, short sentences, normal word order, nothing that calls attention to the writing. At most one light, dry line per page, and only where it helps the reader relax. No jokes that need setting up, no puns, no wordplay, no mannered prose. Assume no expertise.

No mannered prose: the writing must not call attention to itself. Make no assumption that the reader is an expert.

## How to write it

- Regular English, "you" for the reader. Point first, then the reason ("X, so Y"; "When X, do Y").
- Build one idea at a time. No paragraph by list: a list goes in a table.
- Each part has one name, used every time (the names in the plan and the headings).
- Rows must read on their own: no sentence in a table cell starts with "It", "Its", "They" or "This".
- No quotes, no names of people, no brand praise or blame. Makers may be named only where the plan or sources need it to say who advises what (for example "Bosch and Miele say…"), and only plainly.
- No self-narration ("this guide", "this page", "below", "here's", "let's"), no rhetorical questions, no "not X but Y" flourishes, no em dashes, no semicolons, no exclamation marks, no rhythm triplets, no slogans, no kicker endings.
- Avoid: simply, just, really, actually, key, important, crucial, note that.

## Facts to watch (all from sources.md)

- Scrape, don't rinse: every maker in the sources (Bosch, Miele, Whirlpool, GE) and ENERGY STAR say don't pre-rinse under the tap; scrape off large food, bones and toothpicks. Reasons: rinsing adds water and energy (Miele, ENERGY STAR); detergents use enzymes that break down starch and protein (Bosch, Miele, Cascade); some dishwashers sense how dirty the water is and adjust the cycle (Bosch). If dishes will sit overnight, use the dishwasher's rinse cycle instead (ENERGY STAR). Don't use the "up to 20 gallons" figure: only a detergent maker gives it.
- Water sprays from arms under the racks, and the water source is usually in the middle: face plates and the soiled side of bowls toward the middle and pots and pans open side down (Cascade, GE, Frigidaire, Bosch).
- Bottom rack: plates, pots, pans and big items; plates on their side between the tines; big platters along the sides or back so they don't block the spray arm or the detergent dispenser (GE, Frigidaire). Miele puts very large plates in the middle, so say makers differ on big items if you mention placement beyond "not in front of the dispenser".
- Top rack: cups, glasses, small bowls and plastics, open side down and at an angle so water runs off; glasses not touching each other; top rack is cooler, which protects plastic (Cascade). GE and Frigidaire say glasses go only in the top rack; Miele allows them below.
- Cutlery basket: mix the pieces so they don't nest, because water can't reach nested items (Frigidaire, GE, Bosch). Every maker agrees knives go point down. Handles up or down for forks and spoons is where makers disagree: Miele says handles down comes out cleaner and drier but handles up is safer; GE has pages saying each; Bosch's newer manual says all points down; Whirlpool says knives down, alternate spoons. Tie-breaker: your own manual. Don't put sterling silver next to stainless steel (GE, Frigidaire).
- Spray arm: nothing hanging below a rack or sticking up too tall; turn it by hand before you start to check it spins (Miele, Frigidaire).
- Detergent: automatic dishwasher detergent only, never hand dish soap, which makes too much foam (Bosch, Miele, Whirlpool). Put tablets and pods in the dispenser and close the lid (Whirlpool); one tablet is enough (Bosch). Nothing should stop the dispenser lid from opening. Rinse aid helps drying and reduces spots (Bosch, Miele, Whirlpool).
- Don't overload or nest: crowded dishes come out with food left on (Bosch troubleshooting). Do run full loads: a dishwasher uses about the same water and energy however full (ENERGY STAR).
- A dishwasher generally uses less water and energy than washing by hand (Miele, ENERGY STAR: an ENERGY STAR model uses less than half the energy and saves 8,400 gallons a year).
- Run the hot tap at the nearest sink until hot before starting (Whirlpool, older Bosch).
- Not in the dishwasher: wooden items, plastics that aren't heat-resistant, copper and tin (Bosch, Miele). GE says dishwasher-safe wood can go in the top rack, so say "unless marked dishwasher-safe" if you mention wood.
- The study (Journal of Extension, 2003) says pre-rinsing is unnecessary; use it only if you need it.
