# What are the<br/>five problems? {#why}

Five problems turn up often in casual games. Each has a usual way out, and a habit that
helps you avoid it next time.

Moves are written in algebraic notation, explained in An Accidental Guide to: The First
Move in Chess. In 4…Bd7, the dots mark a move by Black. An x marks a capture and + marks
check. Each board is drawn from the side in trouble.

> | Term | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | Pin | A piece can’t move safely because a more valuable one is behind it. |
> | Fork | One piece attacks two or more pieces at once. |
> | Back-rank mate | A rook or queen mates a king shut in behind its own pawns. |
> | Hanging piece | An unprotected piece is open to capture. |
> | King in the center | A king that hasn’t castled when lines toward it open. |

# Pin {#entry num=01 mono=Pn}

```diagram
view black
board After 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 d6 4.d4 | r1bqkbnr/ppp2ppp/2np4/1B2p3/3PP3/5N2/PPP2PPP/RNBQK2R | Black to move. White’s bishop lines up the knight on c6 with the king on e8.
mark Pn c6
arrow Pn b5 e8
```

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | A bishop, rook or queen lined up through your piece to a more valuable one. |
| What | A pin means a piece can’t move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it. The knight on c6 is pinned to the king, so it can’t move at all. |
| So what | Block the line with 4…Bd7. Other ways out are moving the king, or capturing or chasing the pinning piece. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Way out | Block the line |
> | Next time | Check what is lined up |

## Confused with: Relative pin

With the king behind, the pin is absolute. In a relative pin, the pinned piece may move,
but the piece behind it can then be captured.

# Fork {#entry num=02 mono=Fk}

```diagram
view white
board White to move | 4k3/pp3ppp/8/8/8/8/PPn2PPP/R2QK2R | The knight on c2 checks the king and attacks the rook on a1.
mark Fk c2
arrow Fk c2 e1
arrow Fk c2 a1
```

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | One piece, often a knight, attacking two or more of yours. |
| What | A fork is one piece attacking two or more pieces at once. Knights fork well because no piece a knight attacks can capture it, except another knight. |
| So what | Capture the forking piece if you can, here with Qxc2. If you can’t, answer the check and expect to lose the rook. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Way out | Capture the forking piece |
> | Next time | Watch enemy knight jumps |

## Confused with: Pin

A fork attacks two or more pieces at once. A pin stops one piece from moving safely.

# Back-rank mate {#entry num=03 mono=Bk}

```diagram
view white
board White to move | 4r1k1/pp3ppp/8/8/8/Q7/PP3PPP/6K1 | Black threatens Re1. The rook would cover f1 and h1, and the king’s own pawns block the rest.
mark Bk e1
arrow Bk e8 e1
```

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | Your king behind its own pawns, with an enemy rook or queen able to reach its rank. |
| What | A back-rank mate is checkmate by a rook or queen along the back rank, the row where the king started. The king’s own pawns block its escape. |
| So what | Make an escape square with h3, so the king can step to h2, or keep a rook or queen guarding the back rank. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Way out | Give the king a square |
> | Next time | Guard the back rank |

## Confused with: A safe castled king

Castling usually makes your king safer. The pawns that shelter it can also trap it on the
back rank.

# Hanging piece {#entry num=04 mono=Hg}

```diagram
view white
board After 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nf6 3.Nxe5 d6 | rnbqkb1r/ppp2ppp/3p1n2/4N3/4P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNBQKB1R | White to move. The pawn on d6 attacks the knight on e5, and nothing protects the knight.
mark Hg e5
arrow Hg d6 e5
```

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | A piece that nothing defends, with an enemy piece attacking it. |
| What | A hanging piece is unprotected and can be captured, like the knight on e5. |
| So what | Move the piece to a safe square or defend it. Here, move the knight back with 4.Nf3, because a pawn attacks it. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Way out | Move it or defend it |
> | Next time | Check what is unprotected |

## Confused with: En prise

En prise covers any piece that can be taken at a loss, including one with too few
defenders or one attacked by a cheaper piece.

# King in the center {#entry num=05 mono=Ks}

```diagram
view white
board After 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6 5.d4 exd4 6.cxd4 Bb4+ | r1bqk2r/pppp1ppp/2n2n2/8/1bBPP3/5N2/PP3PPP/RNBQK2R | White to move. The bishop on b4 checks the king on e1 before White has castled.
mark Ks e1
arrow Ks b4 e1
```

| Field | Text |
|---|---|
| Identify by | Your king still on e1 or e8, with open lines toward it. |
| What | A king in the center is a king that hasn’t castled when lines toward it open. With the c-pawn and d-pawn gone, the bishop on b4 reaches e1. |
| So what | Block the check with 7.Bd2 or 7.Nc3, bringing a piece into play, then castle. |

## Field marks

> | Field | Short answer |
> |---|---|
> | Way out | Block, then castle |
> | Next time | Castle early |

## Confused with: Castling out of check

You can’t castle while in check, or across or onto a square an enemy piece attacks.
