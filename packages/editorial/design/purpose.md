# What the Accidental Guides are for

Notes for the author, not text for a guide. Based on the catalog and the covers and first
pages of the seven guides as of 22 October 2026.

## (a) Why "Accidental Intelligence"?

The guides weren't planned as a series. The first one, Agents, Harnesses, Skills, and Tools,
was written to settle which word to use for each part of a coding agent, and its running
footer was "Accidental Guide to AI". The others followed from it. "Accidental Intelligence"
is the series' name for itself: guides about artificial intelligence that came about by
accident.

Keep it. It's short, people remember it, and the catalog can explain it in one sentence.
The risk is a reader taking it to mean AI that works by accident, so the catalog should
say where the name comes from on its first page. If you'd rather not keep it, two plain
alternatives:

- The Accidental Guides
- Which Accidental Guide Do I Need?

## (b) Why read these guides when the world is full of information?

Most of what's written about AI coding agents is long, comes from someone selling a
product, and uses the same word for different things ("agent", "lights out", "10x"). Each
guide answers one question, uses one name for each thing and keeps it across the whole
series, and gives the same kind of part the same color in every guide. A reader can pick up
one guide, get a plain answer, and know which guide to read next.

## (c) What the guides are designed to do for the reader

Give someone who is hearing about AI coding agents a plain answer to one question in a few
minutes, with words they can use with their team and a pointer to the next guide.

## One thing to know

The five-minute target (about 1,100 words) is met by the catalog only. The other guides run
from about 2,100 words (Specs and Reviews, Lights-Off Software Factories) to about 4,550
(Agents, Harnesses, Skills, and Tools; Harness Engineering; Running a Software Factory;
Software Factory Claims), so the catalog doesn't promise five minutes per guide yet.
