Accidental GuidesDesign study · 22 October 2026

Covers for the
Accidental Guides

Brian’s question. Do the engravings fit the modern typesetting, colour and other elements of the guides? Do they add to or take away from the unity of the design? Is there a better option, more Swiss/Bauhaus, with a modern take?

Do they fit?

Not on the covers. They’re good pictures in the wrong system. They come from another century, they’re drawn in hatched grey against flat colour, and their subjects are metaphors you have to explain. Each one floats on its own, not on the grid.

Do they help unity?

They help one thing: today every cover is the same white card at thumbnail size, and the engravings at least make them different. But they do it by adding a second visual language, so the series looks less like one thing, not more.

What instead?

B, the part grid. Each cover puts its own coloured tiles on a fixed six-column grid, one column per family. The art comes from the guide’s content, costs nothing per guide, and shows readers the colour rule on every cover. Keep the engravings as a reference, on the about page.

01 · Critique

The engravings are charming, and they’re not part of this system

The guides are already a Swiss system: Helvetica, a strict margin, flat saturated tiles, one meaning per colour, grey blocks, no ornament. Here’s a real cover today and the same cover with its engraving, placed where the sample covers put it.

Where they add

  • Identity at a glance. On the site the covers sit at about 120 px wide, and today they’re identical (below). An engraving is the first thing that makes one cover different from the next.
  • The joke. Babbage’s engine for the AI catalog, the chess Turk (a machine with a person hidden inside) for Games, a magnifying glass for checking claims. That’s the series’ tone: a little dry, a little self-mocking.
  • Craft. Steel and wood engraving is beautiful line work, and it’s free to use.

Where they clash

  • Era. 1783 to 1911 against a 1957 typeface and 2020s Figma colours. O’Reilly made engravings work with a period serif. Next to Helvetica they read as an illustration somebody chose, not part of the page.
  • Line density. Engravings get their tone from hatching, about 30 to 60% grey. The tiles are flat and saturated. On one page they fight, and at thumbnail size the dense plates (Babbage, the loom, the Turk) go to grey smudges.
  • Colour. In this series colour means one thing, the part’s family. The engravings are the only element with no colour and no meaning, and tinting them would give colour a second job.
  • The grid. Each image is a different shape, scale and viewpoint, floating right-aligned in the space between subtitle and tiles. A grid can hold images (Marber, Unigrid), but only if every image gets the same field.
  • Meaning. They show groups, not guides, so five harness guides share one typewriter. The metaphors need a caption: a theodolite for project management, a brace and bit for extending an agent.
  • Supply. Each new group means another archive search, a licence check, a cleanup pass and judgement calls. Two picks already carry a caution (the Turk’s costume, a face on the vaporiser).

02 · Directions

Four systems, each built to scale to dozens of guides

Every mockup uses the guides’ real page (324 × 540 pt), fonts, tile drawing, titles, subtitles, parts, families and page numbers, rendered with ReportLab at 2×. Only the art and the layout change. A, B and D share one new line at the top: the collection’s mark, its name and the guide’s number.

Shared: collection marks

Colour already means a part’s family, so collections shouldn’t get colours of their own. Today the site does give them colours (Accidental Intelligence blue, Around the house yellow, At the table red, Games green), which makes yellow mean both “things that run” and “the house”. Instead, each collection gets a black Bauhaus primary: ■ Accidental Intelligence, ▲ Around the house, ● At the table, ◆ Games. A fifth collection could take a half-disc or a bar. This is worth doing whichever direction is picked.

E

The engravings, as proposed

The sample covers’ treatment, here applied to these eight guides: the current cover plus an engraving, right-aligned, 27% of the page width (32% for collection pictures), top at 180 pt.

A

Signs

One pictogram per guide, drawn to Aicher’s and Arntz’s rules, in ink on the series’ grey block. The parts become a compact contents list beside it.

Rules
  1. A 12 × 12 unit grid inside a 16-unit field. One stroke weight, 1 unit, square caps.
  2. Only 0°, 45° and 90° lines; circles and arcs centred on grid points.
  3. One ink, no colour: colour stays with the part tiles. Overlaps are cut apart with a 0.75-unit white gap.
  4. On the cover: a grey square two columns wide, bottom-right; contents in the left column.

The signs on their grid

At 32 px:
Cost per new guideOne sign, 20 to 60 minutes of careful drawing in code

Good

The direct answer to “the simpler the illustration, the easier it is to use”: the same sign works on the cover, as a favicon, on a share card, beside a link. It keeps the joke (steam off the pan, the chirp), and the grey squares give the shelf a strong, even beat.

Hard

Concrete subjects draw themselves: an alarm, a pan, a pawn. The AI guides, two-thirds of the series, are abstract. The five harness guides would all be windows and terminals, and words like plugin, skill and software factory drift toward generic icon-library glyphs (a plug, a puzzle piece, a gear). Getting past that takes real drawing time for every guide.

B

The part grid

The cover’s art is the guide itself. Six columns, one per family, always in the same order; one row per entry. Each part’s tile sits where its family meets its entry number, so every guide makes its own pattern on the same grid.

Rules
  1. Six columns across the measure (46 pt each), six rows: the series already caps a guide at six parts.
  2. Column heads are one word per family: Runs, Written, Checks, Used, Decided, Counted. Families the guide covers get a filled swatch, the rest an empty outline (the strip design-direction.md already proposes).
  3. The tiles are today’s two-tone tiles, number in the band. Each name and page sits beside its tile, on the right where it fits, otherwise on the left.
  4. No empty cells are drawn. The structure is the column heads and the alignment, not a ruled grid.

Every guide in the repository today, with no extra work

Cost per new guideNone. The builder draws the cover from the parts it already has.

Good

Unity comes from the rules, not from anyone’s taste: every cover is the same system, by definition. Colour gets more meaning, not a second job; a reader who has seen two covers can read the third. It’s Isotype and Bauhaus without nostalgia, and it looks like nothing else on a shelf. At thumbnail size each cover is a distinct pattern.

Hard

It never pictures the subject: the smoke alarm is in the title, not the art. Sibling guides look alike. It’s the most cerebral of the four, so the warmth has to come from the subtitles and the writing. It also depends on every part having a settled family; four older builders don’t have PART_FAMILY yet.

C

Band and number

Pure typography, Unigrid style. A fixed black band holds the kicker, the collection and the title, set on the band’s foot. A large series number fills the lower field, with the contents beside it.

Rules
  1. Band 176 pt, full bleed; the title’s last line always sits 14 pt above its foot.
  2. The number is the guide’s place in its collection, at 160 pt. The catalog is 00.
  3. Numbers are permanent once given, like Penguin’s, and never reused.
Cost per new guideNone, plus one permanent number

Good

The strongest series identity on a shelf and the cheapest to build. Plain, confident and unmannered. The black band also gives the site and share cards one obvious brand mark.

Hard

A number tells the reader nothing: “11” isn’t a reason to pick a guide. Numbers repeat across collections (three 02s on the shelf below). The band is heavy for a pocket guide meant to calm a nervous reader, and C is the furthest from Brian’s wish for illustration. Don’t use the site’s two-letter guide codes instead: Sp, Sk, Mc and He are already part codes.

D

Engravings, reduced

The hybrid: the same engravings, cropped hard into the same two-column square as A, either duotoned on a solid ground of the guide’s main family colour (lines knocked out white; ink on yellow, as the tiles do) or in ink on the grey block.

Cost per new guideAn archive search, a licence check, a cleanup pass, a crop and a credit line

Good

It fixes the placement problem: every picture gets the same field and a hard edge, as Marber and Unigrid did. The purple theodolite and the grey version are handsome, in a museum-catalogue way.

Against

It fixes the grid but not the era, the density or the metaphors. A large field of family colour says “this guide is purple”, which breaks the one-meaning rule colors.md was written to protect. Four of these eight guides come out yellow, the weakest ground. Hard crops lose the silhouette that made the engravings readable. And duotoned archive art is a current fashion that will date quickly. I’d argue against it.

05 · Recommendation

Use B, the part grid

It’s the only direction where the cover art is the guide’s own content, and where the series’ one real visual idea, the same kind of part in the same colour everywhere, does the work. It costs nothing per guide, so it scales to dozens and to new collections by itself. It also fulfils a decision already made in design-direction.md: show which families a guide covers on its cover.

Why not A?

A is the close second, and the right choice if Brian wants a picture on every cover. Its rule book is in scripts/pictograms.py. But two-thirds of the series is about abstract software, and that’s where pictograms turn into stock icons and where the per-guide drawing time goes. If A is chosen, budget for it: every new guide needs a sign drawn to the rules before it ships.

What it costs in the builders

  • build_guide.py: in page_cover(), replace the three-column tile loop with the grid: six column heads, one tile per entry at (family, row), and a label beside it. About 40 lines, using avatar(), FAMILY_TOKEN and PART_FAMILY, which already exist. Add the series line (collection mark, name, number), which needs the collection id and number in each guide’s content or read from collections.json. About half a day in packages/template, plus a check that six rows of 46 pt clear a three-line title and two-line subtitle (they do, with 40 pt to spare).
  • Rolling out: every guide carries its own copy of the builder, so the change goes to 24 build_guide.py files, with new expected.sha256 files and a patch version each. Four older builders (Agents, Claude Code, Claude Desktop, Software Factories) need PART_FAMILY first, the family migration colors.md already describes.
  • build_docx.py: the Word cover becomes a 6 × 6 table with shaded cells. Simple, but cover_gap() needs redoing.
  • The site needs no change for covers: guide_pages.py makes cover.png and card.png from each PDF’s first page. Optionally, use the 6 × 6 pattern as a small mark in lists.
  • Mockup code: scripts/covers.py, cover_grid(), is a working ReportLab prototype on the guides’ constants. Port it, don’t reinvent it.

Where the engravings live

Not on the covers and not in the guides. Give them one place where era and wit are the point: the site’s about page, as a short “From the archive” strip of the machines that came before (Babbage’s engine, the Turk, the loom), each with its credit line. The joke survives there without fighting the system. The 404 page could take one too. Everything is already in engravings/clean with full records in shortlist.json. If even that feels like decoration, leave them out entirely; the guides lose nothing.

What Brian needs to decide

  1. B, or A. The part grid as recommended, or signs if a picture on every cover matters more than zero cost per guide.
  2. Collection marks in black, not colour. Swap the site’s collection colours for ■ ▲ ● ◆, so yellow stops meaning both “things that run” and “the house”.
  3. A guide number on the cover? If yes, numbers are permanent and never reused. B and A work without them.
  4. The six column words: Runs, Written, Checks, Used, Decided, Counted. Short enough for 46 pt, and they work for bread as well as for MCP.
  5. The engravings: about page and 404, or nowhere.
How this was made

All covers were drawn by scripts/covers.py with ReportLab on the guides’ page size, margins, TeX Gyre Heros and two-tone tile, then rendered at 2× with pypdfium2. Titles, subtitles, parts, families and page numbers come from the repository by scripts/data.py (read only). Family colours follow packages/editorial/registry.md. The signs were drawn for this study on a 12-unit grid in scripts/pictograms.py; nothing was traced. The “Now” covers are redrawn the same way so every comparison uses one renderer.

Engravings are public domain; sources and licences are in ~/Claude/accidental-guides/engravings/shortlist.json (Figuier, Les Merveilles de la science; Scammell’s Cyclopedia, 1897; Popular Science Monthly, 1891; Encyclopaedia Britannica, 1911; Harper’s, 1864). TeX Gyre Heros is under the GUST Font License, included in fonts/.
